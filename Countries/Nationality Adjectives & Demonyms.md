# Country Names, Nationality Adjectives and Demonyms in British English

Advanced Grammar and Vocabulary Guide — C1–C2

## 1. The three essential categories

English distinguishes between a country's name, the adjective associated with it, and the noun used to refer to a person from that country. The noun for an inhabitant is called a demonym.

| Category              | Function                           | Example  | British IPA |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------- | ----------- |
| Country name          | Names a country                    | Germany  | /ˈdʒɜːməni/ |
| Nationality adjective | Describes a person, object or idea | German   | /ˈdʒɜːmən/  |
| Demonym               | Names a person from a place        | a German | /ˈdʒɜːmən/  |

The three forms do not always resemble one another.

Compare these examples:

- She lives in Germany. — Country name.
- She is German. — Nationality adjective used after the verb be.
- She is a German. — Nationality word used as a singular countable noun.
- German engineering is highly regarded. — Adjective describing a field.
- The Germans have a rich cultural heritage. — Plural demonym referring to a group.

**Important**: Nationality adjectives and demonyms are not always interchangeable. You can say French cuisine and a French person, but not normally a French when referring to one person.

## 2. The main spelling patterns

There is no universal rule for deriving a nationality adjective from a country name. However, recognising common suffixes makes the vocabulary easier to learn. Treat the following as patterns rather than infallible rules.

### 2.1. The suffix -an and -ian

These are common endings, particularly in names associated with the Americas, Africa, Asia and Europe.

| Country      | Country IPA (BrE) | Adjective / demonym | IPA (BrE)        |
| ------------ | ----------------- | ------------------- | ---------------- |
| America      | /əˈmerɪkə/        | American            | /əˈmerɪkən/      |
| Australia    | /ɒˈstreɪliə/      | Australian          | /ɒˈstreɪliən/    |
| Brazil       | /brəˈzɪl/         | Brazilian           | /brəˈzɪliən/     |
| Canada       | /ˈkænədə/         | Canadian            | /kəˈneɪdiən/     |
| Egypt        | /ˈiːdʒɪpt/        | Egyptian            | /ɪˈdʒɪpʃən/      |
| India        | /ˈɪndiə/          | Indian              | /ˈɪndiən/        |
| Italy        | /ˈɪtəli/          | Italian             | /ɪˈtæliən/       |
| Norway       | /ˈnɔːweɪ/         | Norwegian           | /nɔːˈwiːdʒən/    |
| Russia       | /ˈrʌʃə/           | Russian             | /ˈrʌʃən/         |
| South Africa | /ˌsaʊθ ˈæfrɪkə/   | South African       | /ˌsaʊθ ˈæfrɪkən/ |

Examples:

- She is Australian. /ɒˈstreɪliən/
- He is an Egyptian. /ɪˈdʒɪpʃən/
- They are Canadian. /kəˈneɪdiən/
- Brazilian literature has attracted international attention. /brəˈzɪliən/

C2 distinction: The spelling -ian does not guarantee a predictable pronunciation. For example, Italian and Norwegian have different stress patterns. Learn the whole word, including its stress.

### 2.2. The suffix -ish

This suffix occurs in several well-established nationality adjectives.

| Country | Country IPA (BrE) | Adjective | IPA (BrE)  |
| ------- | ----------------- | --------- | ---------- |
| Britain | /ˈbrɪtən/         | British   | /ˈbrɪtɪʃ/  |
| England | /ˈɪŋɡlənd/        | English   | /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/  |
| Finland | /ˈfɪnlənd/        | Finnish   | /ˈfɪnɪʃ/   |
| Ireland | /ˈaɪələnd/        | Irish     | /ˈaɪrɪʃ/   |
| Poland  | /ˈpəʊlənd/        | Polish    | /ˈpəʊlɪʃ/  |
| Spain   | /speɪn/           | Spanish   | /ˈspænɪʃ/  |
| Sweden  | /ˈswiːdən/        | Swedish   | /ˈswiːdɪʃ/ |
| Turkey  | /ˈtɜːki/          | Turkish   | /ˈtɜːkɪʃ/  |

Examples:

- British universities attract students from all over the world.
- She is Finnish, but her husband is Swedish.
- Spanish architecture varies considerably from region to region.

Notice that Spain becomes Spanish, not Spainish, and Sweden becomes Swedish, not Swedenish.

### 2.3. The suffix -ese

This ending is particularly common in nationality adjectives associated with East Asia and several other regions.

| Country  | Country IPA (BrE) | Adjective  | IPA (BrE)      |
| -------- | ----------------- | ---------- | -------------- |
| China    | /ˈtʃaɪnə/         | Chinese    | /ˌtʃaɪˈniːz/   |
| Japan    | /dʒəˈpæn/         | Japanese   | /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/  |
| Portugal | /ˈpɔːtʃʊɡəl/      | Portuguese | /ˌpɔːtʃʊˈɡiːz/ |
| Vietnam  | /ˌvjetˈnæm/       | Vietnamese | /ˌvjetnəˈmiːz/ |
| Sudan    | /suːˈdɑːn/        | Sudanese   | /ˌsuːdɑːˈniːz/ |

Examples:

- Chinese art has a long and complex history.
- She is a Japanese person.
- Portuguese is spoken in several countries.

Crucial grammatical point: When these words refer to a single person, do not normally use the adjective alone with a/an.

- Correct: She is Chinese.
- Correct: She is a Chinese person.
- Incorrect in standard contemporary usage: She is a Chinese.

For the whole population, use the Chinese, or the more explicit Chinese people. We will return to this distinction in Section 4.

### 2.4. The suffix -i

This ending occurs in several nationalities, especially in South and West Asia.

| Country      | Country IPA (BrE) | Adjective / demonym   | IPA (BrE)                    |
| ------------ | ----------------- | --------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Bangladesh   | /ˌbæŋɡləˈdeʃ/     | Bangladeshi           | /ˌbæŋɡləˈdeʃi/               |
| Iraq         | /ɪˈrɑːk/          | Iraqi                 | /ɪˈrɑːki/                    |
| Israel       | /ˈɪzriəl/         | Israeli               | /ɪzˈreɪli/                   |
| Pakistan     | /ˌpækɪˈstɑːn/     | Pakistani             | /ˌpækɪˈstɑːni/               |
| Saudi Arabia | /ˌsaʊdi əˈreɪbiə/ | Saudi / Saudi Arabian | /ˈsaʊdi/; /ˌsaʊdi əˈreɪbiən/ |

Examples:

- He is Iraqi.
- She is a Pakistani.
- Saudi Arabian oil exports play a significant role in global energy markets.

Do not assume that every nationality ending in -i follows the same pattern in every context. In particular, Saudi and Saudi Arabian are both used, but their distribution depends on the noun being described and the intended meaning.

### 2.5. Other recurring endings

| Pattern | Country | Adjective | IPA (BrE)      |
| ------- | ------- | --------- | -------------- |
| -ese    | Lebanon | Lebanese  | /ˌlebəˈniːz/   |
| -ese    | Taiwan  | Taiwanese | /ˌtaɪwɑːˈniːz/ |

| -ian    | Hungary | Hungarian | /hʌŋˈɡeəriən/  |

| -an     | Korea   | Korean    | /kəˈriːən/     |

These patterns overlap. For example, Icelandic is not formed with -an or -ian, despite the prevalence of those endings elsewhere.

## 3. Irregular forms: the most important exceptions

These are particularly important at C1–C2 because the country name alone does not reliably predict the adjective or the word for an individual.

### 3.1. Why the adjective and the demonym differ

Consider the following pairs:

- Denmark → Danish → a Dane
- Finland → Finnish → a Finn
- Poland → Polish → a Pole
- Spain → Spanish → a Spaniard
- Sweden → Swedish → a Swede
- Turkey → Turkish → a Turk

The adjective describes a person, object or characteristic associated with a country. The demonym identifies an individual.

Compare:

- Danish design is admired internationally.
- She is Danish.
- She is a Dane.

All three sentences are grammatical, but the final sentence uses a noun rather than an adjective.

### 3.2. France, Greece and the Netherlands

These three cases deserve special attention.

France → French

- French wine is popular worldwide.
- He is French.
- He is a French person.

Avoid a French when referring to one individual in ordinary contemporary English.

Greece → Greek

- Greek philosophy influenced Western thought.
- She is Greek.
- She is a Greek.

Unlike French, Greek can be used directly as a singular countable noun.

The Netherlands → Dutch

- Dutch law has its own distinctive features.
- He is Dutch.
- He is a Dutch person.

The form Netherlandish exists in specialised historical and art-historical contexts, but it is not the ordinary modern nationality adjective. Use Dutch for the nationality.

Also, Holland is not strictly synonymous with the Netherlands: Holland refers to two provinces within the country, although it is sometimes used informally for the whole country.

## 4. How to refer to one person or a whole population

This is one of the most important grammatical distinctions.

### 4.1. Nationality words that can be singular nouns

For many nationalities, the adjective can also function as a countable noun referring to an individual.

| Adjective  | Singular noun | Plural noun |
| ---------- | ------------- | ----------- |
| American   | an American   | Americans   |
| Australian | an Australian | Australians |
| Brazilian  | a Brazilian   | Brazilians  |
| Canadian   | a Canadian    | Canadians   |
| German     | a German      | Germans     |
| Indian     | an Indian     | Indians     |
| Italian    | an Italian    | Italians    |
| Russian    | a Russian     | Russians    |

Examples:

- She is Italian.
- She is an Italian.
- Italians have contributed enormously to European art and music.

The adjective in She is Italian does not need an article. The singular countable noun in She is an Italian does.

### 4.2. Nationalities ending in -ese, -ish and related forms

Many nationality adjectives cannot normally be used as singular countable nouns without an additional noun such as person or woman.

| Adjective  | Singular person               | Collective expression |
| ---------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------- |
| British    | a British person              | the British           |
| Chinese    | a Chinese person              | the Chinese           |
| Japanese   | a Japanese person             | the Japanese          |
| Portuguese | a Portuguese person           | the Portuguese        |
| Spanish    | a Spanish person / a Spaniard | the Spanish           |
| French     | a French person               | the French            |
| Dutch      | a Dutch person                | the Dutch             |
| Welsh      | a Welsh person                | the Welsh             |

Examples:

- A Japanese person joined our research team.
- The Japanese are represented in the study by several demographic groups.
- Many French people support the preservation of regional traditions.
- The French have a rich literary tradition.

The grammatical distinction is that the + nationality adjective can denote a people as a collective group, whereas a/an + adjective usually requires a singular countable noun.

### 4.3. The difference between the British and British people

Both are grammatical, but their emphasis differs slightly.

- The British are often portrayed in international media in particular ways. — A collective expression referring to the British population.
- British people have diverse regional identities and cultural backgrounds. — An explicit plural noun phrase.

Prefer British people, French citizens or Japanese respondents when you need to be precise about the population under discussion.

A broad collective expression such as the British can sound sweeping if it is used to generalise about millions of individuals.

### 4.4. A note on -man and -woman

Some traditional demonyms have gender-specific forms:

- an Englishman / an Englishwoman
- a Frenchman / a Frenchwoman
- a Dutchman / a Dutchwoman
- an Irishman / an Irishwoman
- a Welshman / a Welshwoman

In contemporary formal writing, an English person, a French person and similar expressions are often preferable when gender is irrelevant. They are also useful for avoiding assumptions about gender.

## 5. Britain, the United Kingdom and England are not interchangeable

This distinction is essential for accurate British English.

The United Kingdom (the UK)

/ðə ˌjuːˈkeɪ/

Nationality adjective: British /ˈbrɪtɪʃ/

A person from England, Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland may identify as British, although individual preferences vary.

England

/ˈɪŋɡlənd/

Nationality adjective: English /ˈɪŋɡlɪʃ/

Demonym: an English person, an Englishman or an Englishwoman.

Scotland

/ˈskɒtlənd/

Nationality adjective: Scottish /ˈskɒtɪʃ/

Demonym: a Scot /skɒt/ or a Scottish person.

Wales

/weɪlz/

Nationality adjective: Welsh /welʃ/

Demonym: a Welsh person.

Compare these statements:

- She is British, but she is not English; she is Scottish.
- Welsh is a language, whereas Welsh is also an adjective describing people and things associated with Wales.
- English is widely spoken around the world, but British does not mean English-speaking.

The last point is especially important: English can refer to a language, a school subject, or something associated with England. British refers to Britain or the United Kingdom, depending on context.

## 6. Capitalisation rules

In English, country names, nationality adjectives, demonyms and language names begin with a capital letter.

| Incorrect              | Correct                |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------- |
| german engineering     | German engineering     |
| she speaks french      | She speaks French.     |
| an italian architect   | an Italian architect   |
| the british government | the British government |
| japanese literature    | Japanese literature    |

This rule applies even when the nationality adjective appears in the middle of a sentence.

## 7. Nationality adjectives and language names

The nationality adjective often shares its form with the language name, but this is not universal.

| Country     | Nationality adjective  | Language                                                |
| ----------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Germany     | German /ˈdʒɜːmən/      | German /ˈdʒɜːmən/                                       |
| Italy       | Italian /ɪˈtæliən/     | Italian /ɪˈtæliən/                                      |
| Japan       | Japanese /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/ | Japanese /ˌdʒæpəˈniːz/                                  |
| Brazil      | Brazilian /brəˈzɪliən/ | Portuguese /ˌpɔːtʃʊˈɡiːz/                               |
| Egypt       | Egyptian /ɪˈdʒɪpʃən/   | Arabic /ˈærəbɪk/                                        |
| Switzerland | Swiss /swɪs/           | Swiss German, French, Italian and Romansh, among others |

Examples:

- He is German, and he speaks German.
- She is Brazilian, but Portuguese is her first language.
- He is Egyptian and speaks Arabic.

Advanced point: Nationality does not determine a person's language repertoire. A person may have a nationality without speaking the language traditionally associated with that country.

## 8. Nationality adjectives in formal and academic writing

It is useful to understand how these forms operate in complex noun phrases.

### 8.1. Adjective before a noun

Nationality adjectives commonly modify nouns directly:

- French constitutional law
- German industrial policy
- Japanese literary criticism
- Brazilian economic development
- British parliamentary democracy

The nationality adjective normally comes before the noun it modifies.

### 8.2. Adjective after a linking verb

Nationality adjectives also occur after verbs such as be, seem and remain.

- The new director is Canadian.
- The author appears to be Polish.
- Although born abroad, she remained British.

Do not add an article before the adjective in these constructions.

- Correct: He is French.
- Incorrect: He is a French.

The second sentence would need a noun, as in He is a French citizen.

### 8.3. Noun phrases with more than one nationality-related word

Complex descriptions can contain multiple geographical or national adjectives:

- a British-born Australian novelist
- a French-speaking Canadian researcher
- a German-owned British company
- a Japanese-trained American architect

These expressions do not necessarily indicate legal nationality. For example, French-speaking Canadian identifies a language-related characteristic, while Japanese-trained American indicates training in Japan and American identity.

## 10. Recommended learning strategy

Learn each nationality as a three-part set rather than memorising the country name alone:

1. Country: Spain /speɪn/
2. Adjective: Spanish /ˈspænɪʃ/
3. Person: a Spaniard /ˈspænjəd/

Then add two more pieces of information:

- Collective form: the Spanish
- Language: Spanish

Repeat the process for irregular pairs such as the Netherlands → Dutch → a Dutch person and Sweden → Swedish → a Swede.

The most useful principle is this: learn the adjective, the singular demonym and the collective form separately whenever they differ. This will help you avoid the most persistent errors in advanced writing and speech.
