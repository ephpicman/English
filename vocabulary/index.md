# Idioms

### 5. be the apple of someone's eye
- **Meaning:** To be someone who is loved and cherished very much.
- **Register:** Neutral, slightly traditional or affectionate.
- **Use:** Often used by or about parents, grandparents, and children; it can also describe someone outside the family.
- **Example:** His granddaughter is **the apple of his eye**.
- **Caution:** Warm and affectionate, but somewhat expressive; it may sound sentimental in formal or factual writing.

### 7. get on like a house on fire
- **Meaning:** To become friendly very quickly and get along extremely well.
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Use:** Suitable for describing relatives, in-laws, friends, or people who have just met.
- **Example:** I was nervous about meeting my new sister-in-law, but we **got on like a house on fire**.
- **Caution:** This describes a very strong, quick rapport, not merely a polite relationship.
### 9. have someone wrapped around your little finger
- **Meaning:** To be able to persuade or control someone easily, often because they are very fond of you.
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Frequently used about children who can easily persuade parents or grandparents, but also applies to adults.
- **Example:** Their youngest son **has his grandparents wrapped around his little finger**.
- **Caution:** Often playful, but it can suggest manipulation if the context is negative.
### 12. skeletons in the closet
- **Meaning:** Secrets from someone's past, often embarrassing or shameful, that they do not want others to discover.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; the British English variant is often “skeletons in the cupboard”.
- **Use:** Can refer to family secrets or to a person's private past. Use when the idea of concealed, potentially damaging information matters.
- **Example:** While researching their family history, they discovered a few **skeletons in the cupboard**.
- **Caution:** It suggests potentially troubling secrets, not simply private information. “Closet” is more common in American English; “cupboard” is common in British English.



## Proverbs

### 4. Home is where the heart is.
- **Meaning:** Home is wherever you feel loved, safe, and emotionally connected—not necessarily a particular building or birthplace.
- **Register:** Neutral; warm and sentimental.
- **Use:** Useful when talking about moving, migration, chosen family, or feeling at home in a new place.
- **Example:** We’ve lived in three countries, but wherever we’re together, home is where the heart is.
- **Caution:** It is heartfelt and somewhat sentimental; it may sound too emotional in factual or highly formal writing.

**Spare the rod and spoil the child.**
- **Meaning:** The traditional claim that children need strict discipline or punishment to behave well.
- **Register:** Traditional; often critical or disapproving in modern contexts.
- **Use:** Mainly encountered when discussing older views of parenting or quoting a traditional attitude.
- **Example:** His grandfather believed in “spare the rod and spoil the child,” but the younger parents preferred non-violent discipline.
- **Caution:** This saying is associated with corporal punishment and should not be presented as established parenting advice. Modern use may sound outdated or endorse harmful discipline.

**The proof of the pudding is in the eating.**
- **Meaning:** The real value or success of something can only be judged by trying it or seeing the result.
- **Register:** Neutral; traditional.
- **Use:** Not exclusively about family, but can be used when evaluating parenting approaches, family arrangements, or marriage advice.
- **Example:** Everyone has an opinion about their parenting plan, but the proof of the pudding is in the eating.
- **Caution:** The full traditional form is “the proof of the pudding is in the eating”; the shorter “the proof is in the pudding” is common, especially in American English.

**Marry in haste, repent at leisure.**
- **Meaning:** Rushing into marriage may lead to long-lasting regret.
- **Register:** Traditional; neutral to disapproving.
- **Use:** Said as a warning against making a marriage decision too quickly.
- **Example:** Her aunt urged her to take time to get to know her partner, reminding her that people who marry in haste may repent at leisure.
- **Caution:** Old-fashioned and judgemental in tone; it should not be used to belittle someone’s relationship or assume a quick marriage will fail.

**Happy wife, happy life.**
- **Meaning:** A partner’s happiness—traditionally the wife’s—is presented as important to a peaceful marriage.
- **Register:** Informal; often humorous.
- **Use:** Common as a joking comment about keeping a spouse happy or compromising in a relationship.
- **Example:** He happily went to the furniture shop with his wife, joking, “Happy wife, happy life.”
- **Caution:** It is gendered and can imply that one partner is responsible for the other’s happiness. It is best treated as a familiar slogan, not serious relationship guidance.

**Old habits die hard.**
- **Meaning:** Long-established habits are difficult to change.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Not specifically about families, but often used about family routines, parenting patterns, and behaviours learned while growing up.
- **Example:** He still calls his mother every evening, as he did when he was a student—old habits die hard.
- **Caution:** This is a general proverb that can be applied to family life; it is not exclusively a family proverb.

**There’s no place like home.**
- **Meaning:** Home is uniquely comforting or familiar, especially after time away.
- **Register:** Neutral; traditional and often sentimental.
- **Use:** Said when returning home after travel or describing the comfort of one’s own home.
- **Example:** After six months abroad, she stepped through the front door and sighed, “There’s no place like home.”
- **Caution:** It may not reflect everyone’s experience; home is not safe or welcoming for all people.

## Expressions

be on the waiting list
- **Meaning:** To be waiting for an available place or appointment.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** We've put his name down, but he's still on the waiting list for nursery.
- **Nuance:** Used for nurseries, schools, clubs, and appointments.

settle in
- **Meaning:** To become comfortable in a new place, routine, school, or situation.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** How's your daughter settling in at her new school?
- **Nuance:** A natural question to ask after a child starts somewhere new.

get in touch with the school
- **Meaning:** To contact the school.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** I'll get in touch with the school to let them know she'll be absent.
- **Nuance:** A common, natural phrase for school communication.

have a falling-out
- **Meaning:** To have an argument that damages a relationship.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** The sisters had a falling-out over their parents' house.
- **Nuance:** It may be temporary or long-lasting; the phrase doesn't say whether they later reconciled.

patch things up
- **Meaning:** To improve or repair a relationship after an argument.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Example:** They've finally patched things up after months of not speaking.
- **Nuance:** Suggests reconciliation, but not necessarily that every underlying issue has been resolved.

fall out with someone
- **Meaning:** To argue and become unfriendly with someone.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** I fell out with my brother over something silly, but we're fine now.
- **Nuance:** In British English, “fall out with” is very natural.

be on speaking terms
- **Meaning:** To be willing to speak to someone, especially after a disagreement.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** They had a huge argument and are barely on speaking terms.
- **Nuance:** “Barely on speaking terms” means they speak very little or only when necessary.

not see eye to eye
- **Meaning:** To disagree with someone.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** My dad and I don't always see eye to eye on politics.
- **Nuance:** Disagreement does not necessarily mean the relationship is bad.

set boundaries
- **Meaning:** To make clear what behaviour you will accept and what limits you need.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal; common in contemporary relationship and parenting discussions.
- **Example:** We've had to set boundaries about relatives dropping by unannounced.
- **Nuance:** Does not automatically mean cutting someone off; it can simply mean agreeing on limits.

cut someone off
- **Meaning:** To stop contact with or financial support for someone; it can also mean interrupting a speaker.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** After years of arguments, she decided to cut her father off.
- **Nuance:** The exact meaning depends on context.

be there for someone
- **Meaning:** To support someone emotionally or practically when they need help.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** Whatever happens, I'll always be there for you.
- **Nuance:** Can mean listening, helping, or simply being available.

lean on someone
- **Meaning:** To rely on someone for emotional or practical support.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** When things got difficult, she leaned on her sister for support.
- **Nuance:** “Lean on someone” can also mean put pressure on them, so context matters.

going through a rough patch
- **Meaning:** Experiencing a difficult period, often in a relationship or family life.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Example:** They're going through a rough patch, but they're trying to work things out.
- **Nuance:** Does not necessarily mean a relationship is ending.

things are a bit up in the air
- **Meaning:** Plans or circumstances are uncertain and not yet settled.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** Our childcare arrangements are a bit up in the air for next week.
- **Nuance:** A useful way to communicate uncertainty without explaining every detail.

be estranged from someone
- **Meaning:** To have little or no relationship with a relative or someone formerly close, often after a conflict.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2–C1
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Example:** She's been estranged from her father for years.
- **Nuance:** More formal than “not speaking to”; it does not say who is responsible or why contact ended.

be a handful
- **Meaning:** To be difficult to manage because someone is very energetic, demanding, or troublesome.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Example:** The twins are a real handful at the moment, but they're lovely kids.
- **Nuance:** Often affectionate or mildly exasperated, rather than a serious criticism.

go through a phase
- **Meaning:** To behave or feel a certain way for a period, often as part of growing up.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** He's going through a phase of refusing to eat vegetables.
- **Nuance:** Often suggests the behaviour is temporary, though that is not guaranteed.

act up
- **Meaning:** To behave badly or disruptively; also, for a problem or device, to cause trouble.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Example:** He was acting up in class yesterday.
- **Nuance:** “My back's acting up” means the back is causing problems. Context determines the meaning.

play up
- **Meaning:** In British English, to behave badly or be difficult; also used when a problem or device causes trouble.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially British English.
- **Example:** The kids were playing up because they were overtired.
- **Nuance:** “My knee's playing up” means my knee is causing problems.

push the boundaries
- **Meaning:** To test rules or limits to find out what is allowed.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** She's been pushing the boundaries to see how much screen time she'll get.
- **Nuance:** Can describe normal child development, not necessarily serious misbehaviour.

settle down
- **Meaning:** To establish a stable life, often by committing to a partner or having children.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Example:** My relatives keep asking when I'm going to settle down.
- **Nuance:** Depending on tone, it can be affectionate teasing or pressure to follow conventional life choices.

be expecting
- **Meaning:** A common indirect way to say that someone is pregnant.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** They're expecting their first baby in December.
- **Nuance:** Often used when announcing pregnancy. Do not assume or announce someone else's pregnancy.

be due in [month] / be due any day now
- **Meaning:** To be expected to give birth around a particular time.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** She's due in early March. / The baby's due any day now.
- **Nuance:** “Due” also refers to deadlines and expected events; context makes the meaning clear.

pass away
- **Meaning:** A gentle, indirect way to say “die”.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to formal; considerate.
- **Example:** Her grandfather passed away last winter.
- **Nuance:** Very common in conversation and messages about bereavement.

lose someone
- **Meaning:** An indirect way to say that someone has died.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral, sensitive.
- **Example:** I'm so sorry to hear you've lost your mum.
- **Nuance:** Context distinguishes this from other meanings of “lose”.

no longer with us
- **Meaning:** A euphemism meaning that someone has died.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Gentle, somewhat formal or ceremonial.
- **Example:** Her grandparents are no longer with us.
- **Nuance:** Understand it when you hear or read it, but “passed away” is often more natural in everyday conversation.

have a bit of a wobble
- **Meaning:** To become temporarily upset, anxious, uncertain, or overwhelmed.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal; British English.
- **Example:** He had a bit of a wobble on his first morning at nursery, but he settled quickly.
- **Nuance:** Mild conversational understatement, not usually a description of a serious mental-health crisis.

it's been a lot
- **Meaning:** An understated way to say that a situation has been emotionally or practically demanding.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; common in contemporary conversation.
- **Example:** The last few weeks have been a lot, to be honest.
- **Nuance:** Often signals that the speaker wants empathy more than advice. The vagueness may be deliberate.

## Phrasal Verbs

cut someone off
- **Meaning:** To stop communicating with or supporting a relative.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** After years of hurtful arguments, he cut off contact with his uncle.
- **Usage note:** “Cut someone off” can also mean interrupt them, so context matters. “Cut off contact with” makes the family meaning explicit.

get on with someone
- **Meaning:** To have a friendly or good relationship with a relative.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** I get on really well with my cousins.
- **Usage note:** “Get along with” is the common equivalent in American English.

all out with someone
- **Meaning:** To have an argument with a relative and become unfriendly.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** He fell out with his brother over their father's will.
- **Usage note:** It describes a disagreement that affects the relationship, not just a brief difference of opinion.

make up with someone
- **Meaning:** To become friendly again with a relative after an argument.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Example:** She made up with her sister after they talked things through.
- **Usage note:** “Make up” alone can have other meanings; “make up with someone” clearly means reconcile.
