# Foster Family

**foster**: `/ˈfɒstə(r)/` - `v. [T]`  
To care for a child in your home, usually temporarily, without becoming the child's adoptive parent.
  - They agreed to foster a child until a permanent placement could be arranged.
  - They agreed to **foster a child** for six months.
  - The couple decided to **foster kittens** while the shelter was full.

**foster parent**: `/ˈfɒstə peərənt/` - `n. [C]`  
A person who provides a home and care for a child who is not their own, usually temporarily.
  - A foster parent provided a safe home while the child's case was reviewed.
  - The child was placed with **approved foster parents**.

**foster child**: `/ˈfɒstə tʃaɪld/` - `n. [C]`  
A child who is being cared for by a foster parent or foster family.
  - The foster child stayed with the same family for two years.

## Practice: Dialogue, Monologue and Reading

### 1. Dialogue: The School Form

**Teacher:** I’m updating the emergency contacts. Should I list your mum and dad?

**Oliver:** Please list my **foster parents**. They’re the people I live with at the moment.

**Teacher:** Of course. And is it okay to call you their **foster child** on the form?

**Oliver:** I’d rather you just use my name. I know what “foster” means, but I don’t want it to be the first thing everyone knows about me.

**Teacher:** That’s fair. I’ll keep the form private and use the wording you’re comfortable with.

**Oliver:** Thanks. My foster parents said they can come to parents’ evening, too.

### 2. Monologue: The First Week

The first week after the placement felt strange for all of us. We had agreed to **foster** a child while the social workers worked out what support would be needed next. We made space in the bedroom, asked about food he liked, and tried to keep the daily routine predictable. We didn’t expect him to trust us immediately, and we avoided asking questions about his past unless he chose to talk. Being a **foster parent** meant offering care without pretending we could decide the whole future. The small routines—walking to school, cooking dinner, leaving a light on—helped the house feel less unfamiliar.

### 3. Reading: Foster Care and Adoption Are Different

To **foster** a child is to provide care, usually for a period of time, when the child cannot safely live with their usual caregivers. A **foster parent** offers a home and day-to-day support, often while professionals and family members consider what arrangement is best for the child. A **foster child** may stay for a short period or much longer, depending on the circumstances. Foster care does not automatically make the caregiver the child’s legal parent.

**Adoption** is different: it is a legal process that usually establishes a permanent parent-child relationship. Foster placements may end in reunification with birth family, a move to another caregiver, or adoption, depending on the child’s needs and the legal process. The phrase “temporary” does not mean the relationships formed during foster care are unimportant. Children need clear explanations, reliable care and adults who respect their history and connections. In this context, “foster” can also be used for animals, as in fostering kittens for a shelter, but the responsibilities and legal arrangements are different.
