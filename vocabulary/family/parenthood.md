# Parenthood

**co-parent**: `/ˌkəʊ ˈpeərənt/` - `n. [C]`

  - They continue to co-parent their daughter after separating.
  - They learned to **co-parent a child after separation**.
  - They often discuss a **co-parenting arrangement**.

**single parent**: `/ˌsɪŋɡl ˈpeərənt/` - `n. [C]`  
A parent who brings up a child without a partner.
  - As a single parent, he manages work and childcare on his own.
  - They often discuss a **single parent household**.
  - They often discuss support for **single parents**.

**single-parent family**: `/ˌsɪŋɡl ˈpeərənt ˌfæməli/` - `n. [C]`  
A family in which one parent brings up the children.
  - The school offers extra support to children from single-parent families.
  - They often discuss children from a **single-parent family**.
  - The local centre works to **support a single-parent family** in need.

**two-parent family**: `/ˌtuː ˈpeərənt ˌfæməli/` - `n. [C]`  
A family in which a child is raised by two parents.
  - The survey included children living in a two-parent family.
  - They often discuss a **two-parent family household**.
  - Many children **grow up in a two-parent family**.

**paternal**: `/pəˈtɜːnl/` - `adj.`

**maternity**: `/məˈtɜːnəti/` - `n. [U]`

## Proverbs and Proverbial Saying

**It takes a village to raise a child.**
- **Meaning:** Raising a child successfully requires support from a wider community, not only the parents.
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Common in discussions of parenting, education, childcare, and community support.
- **Example:** The neighbours, teachers, and grandparents all help the children thrive; it really takes a village to raise a child.
- **Caution:** Often associated with African-origin communal wisdom, but the exact origin and attribution are not straightforward. Use it to emphasise shared support, not to undermine parents’ role.

**As the twig is bent, so grows the tree.**
- **Meaning:** A child’s early upbringing is believed to strongly influence their later character.
- **Register:** Literary; traditional.
- **Use:** Appears in discussions of childhood education, discipline, and formative experiences.
- **Example:** The headteacher quoted “as the twig is bent, so grows the tree” when explaining why early support matters.
- **Caution:** The proverb can overstate how much childhood determines adult life; people can change and develop throughout their lives.

## Phrasal Verbs

**bring someone up**
- **Meaning:** To care for and raise a child.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** She was brought up by her grandparents.
- **Nuance:** “Bring up” can also mean introduce a topic in conversation; the object and context distinguish the meanings.

**bring someone along**
- **Meaning:** To bring a child or family member with you to an event or place.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** You can bring the kids along to the family picnic.
- **Usage note:** The phrase is general-purpose, but this is a common practical use in family plans.

**look after someone**
- **Meaning:** To take care of a child or family member.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2
- **Register:** Neutral; common in British English.
- **Example:** Could you look after the children while I go to the pharmacy?
- **Usage note:** “Look after” is especially common in British English; American English often uses “take care of”.

## Expressions

**babysit for someone**
- **Meaning:** To look after someone's child for a short time while the parent or usual carer is away.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** My brother often babysits for us on Saturday evenings.
- **Usage note:** “Babysit” is the main verb; “sit for” is the phrasal-verb construction.

**take care of someone**
- **Meaning:** To provide the care or help a child or family member needs.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** His grandmother took care of him while his parents were at work.
- **Usage note:** This is a verb phrase rather than a strict phrasal verb, but it is highly relevant to family care.

**juggle work and family**
- **Meaning:** To manage work and family responsibilities at the same time.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** He's finding it hard to juggle work and family while his partner is away.
- **Nuance:** “Juggle” suggests competing demands that require careful management.

**have childcare sorted**
- **Meaning:** To have arranged who will look after a child.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; common in British English.
- **Example:** I can stay late if I've got childcare sorted.
- **Nuance:** “Sorted” means arranged or dealt with.

**family commitments**
- **Meaning:** Family responsibilities or plans that affect when someone is available.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Example:** I'm afraid I can't attend the evening event because of family commitments.
- **Nuance:** Useful at work when you want to explain unavailability without sharing private details.

**be an empty nester**
- **Meaning:** To be a parent whose children have grown up and moved out.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** Now that both children have moved out, they're getting used to being empty nesters.
- **Nuance:** The transition can feel positive, sad, or mixed.

**the school run**
- **Meaning:** The routine of taking children to school or collecting them.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; particularly British English.
- **Example:** I can't make a meeting at three because I have to do the school run.
- **Nuance:** Can refer to the morning drop-off, afternoon collection, or both.

**after-school club**
- **Meaning:** An organised activity or childcare session after the school day.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral; common in the UK.
- **Example:** I've booked her into after-school club on Tuesdays.
- **Nuance:** Common in school messages and conversations between parents.

**wraparound care**
- **Meaning:** Childcare before and/or after normal school hours.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal; common in UK school administration.
- **Example:** Does the school offer wraparound care from half past seven?
- **Nuance:** More likely in school emails, forms, and policies than casual chat.

**start a family**
- **Meaning:** To begin having children.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when people discuss future plans as a couple.
- **Example:** We're hoping to start a family in the next couple of years.
- **Nuance:** It can be a personal or sensitive topic, so avoid assuming everyone wants or can have children.

**be trying for a baby**
- **Meaning:** To be actively trying to conceive a child.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** They're trying for a baby, but they haven't told many people.
- **Nuance:** Useful to understand, but fertility is private; avoid asking people about it unless they raise the subject.

## Practice: Dialogue, Monologue and Reading

### 1. Dialogue: Sorting Out Childcare

**Sophie:** Have you booked the after-school club for Thursday?

**Mark:** Not yet. I need to check whether they offer **wraparound care** on the day I’m on an early shift.

**Sophie:** I can do the **school run** in the morning, but I’ve got a meeting at three.

**Mark:** I’ll ask my sister to **look after** them until I get home.

**Sophie:** Good idea. I’ll check the club can take them on Friday as well, then we’ll have **childcare sorted** for the week.

**Mark:** Thanks. We’re both trying to **juggle work and family** at the moment. I don’t want to leave it all until the last minute again.

### 2. Monologue: What I Learned from My Upbringing

I grew up in a two-parent family, but my grandparents were just as important in my **upbringing**. My **maternal** grandmother taught me to cook, while my **paternal** grandfather showed me how to fix things. Now that I’m a parent, I understand that **motherhood** and **fatherhood** are less about knowing all the answers and more about being willing to learn. My partner and I try to **raise** our children with patience. Some people say **rear** or **bring up** children; the meaning is similar, though “rear” can sound more formal. We don’t always get it right, but we try to put things right when we make mistakes.

### 3. Reading: Parenthood, Care and Family Circumstances

**Parenthood** is the state or experience of being a parent; **parenting** is the day-to-day work of caring for and guiding a child. **Parental** is the adjective used for matters related to parents, including **parental responsibility**, rights and consent. **Paternity** concerns being a father, while **maternity** concerns being a mother and appears in expressions such as maternity leave and maternity care. A **single parent** raises a child without a partner sharing the day-to-day parenting role; a **single-parent family** is organised around one parent. A **two-parent family** has two parents, although family structures and responsibilities vary. Parents who are separated may still **co-parent**, sharing decisions and care.

To **raise** or **bring up** a child is to care for them as they grow; **rear** is a similar, somewhat more formal verb. A **neglectful parent** fails to provide the care a child needs. Other adults may **take care of** children, **babysit for** a parent or **look after** them for a short time. Parents may **juggle work and family**, manage **family commitments**, arrange an **after-school club** or **wraparound care**, and make sure they **have childcare sorted**. The **school run** means taking children to or collecting them from school. **Bring someone along** means to take that person with you, as in “You can bring the children along to the picnic.” When children leave home, their parents may become **empty nesters**. Adults may talk about wanting to **start a family** or **trying for a baby**, but these are personal matters and shouldn’t be assumed or pressed.

The saying **“It takes a village to raise a child”** highlights the role of community support. **“As the twig is bent, so grows the tree”** suggests that early experiences influence later character, though they do not determine a person’s future. These sayings are useful to recognise, but neither should be treated as a complete theory of child development.
