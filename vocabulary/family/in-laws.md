# In-laws

**mother-in-law**: `/ˈmʌðər ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The mother of your husband or wife.
  - We invited my mother-in-law to stay for the weekend.
  - She gets on well with her **future mother-in-law**.

**father-in-law**: `/ˈfɑːðər ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The father of your husband or wife.
  - Her father-in-law helped them move into their new house.
  - They invited her **future father-in-law** to dinner.

**son-in-law**: `/ˈsʌn ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The husband of your daughter.
  - Their son-in-law prepared dinner for the whole family.
  - She welcomed her new **son-in-law** into the family.

**daughter-in-law**: `/ˈdɔːtər ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The wife of your son.
  - My daughter-in-law sent us photographs of the baby.
  - They invited their **future daughter-in-law** to lunch.

**brother-in-law**: `/ˈbrʌðər ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The brother of your husband or wife, or the husband of your sibling.
  - Her brother-in-law is married to her younger sister.
  - She went on holiday with her **brother-in-law**.

**sister-in-law**: `/ˈsɪstər ɪn lɔː/` - `n. [C]`  
The sister of your husband or wife, or the wife of your sibling.
  - I went shopping with my sister-in-law.
  - She and her **sister-in-law** share the childcare.

## Proverbs

**A son is a son till he gets him a wife; a daughter’s a daughter all her life.**
- **Meaning:** This old saying claims that a son may become more attached to his wife’s household after marriage, while a daughter remains loyal to her birth family.
- **Register:** Old-fashioned; culturally specific.
- **Use:** Mostly encountered in literature, family discussions, or as a quotation of traditional gender expectations.
- **Example:** At the family gathering, her grandmother quoted the old saying about sons and daughters, and the younger relatives challenged its assumptions.
- **Caution:** Strongly gendered and not a reliable description of family relationships. Use it to discuss or quote a traditional belief, not as a neutral fact.

## Practice: Dialogue, Monologue and Reading

### 1. Dialogue: The Sunday Lunch

**Emma:** Mum’s asked us over for lunch on Sunday. Are we free?

**James:** I thought we were seeing my parents on Sunday.

**Emma:** No, Saturday. You put it in the calendar yourself.

**James:** Did I? Right. I’ll check with Mum before I promise anything. My brother-in-law might be staying with us that weekend, too.

**Emma:** Your brother-in-law or mine?

**James:** Yours, Paul. Sorry. Family titles are a minefield.

**Emma:** Just use his name, then. It’ll save us both a headache.

**James:** Fair point. I’ll ring my mum and confirm Saturday.

### 2. Monologue: Our First Holiday Together

I was nervous about spending a week away with my in-laws because I’d only ever seen them at birthdays and Christmas. At home, you can escape to the kitchen or say you’ve got an early start. On holiday, you’re all sharing a cottage and there’s nowhere to hide when someone gets on your nerves. The first day was awkward: my father-in-law wanted to set off at seven, my mother-in-law had packed enough food for a month, and my sister-in-law thought we should decide everything as we went along. Then it rained for two days, the power went off, and we ended up playing cards by candlelight. We laughed more that evening than we had all week. We weren’t suddenly best friends, but we stopped trying so hard to impress one another.

### 3. Reading: The Seat at the Table

When Daniel married, he expected the usual learning curve: remembering birthdays, finding out who took sugar in their tea and working out which stories had already been told. What he hadn’t expected was the annual argument over where everyone should spend Christmas. His wife’s parents assumed the couple would come to them; his own mum felt that, as her only son, he ought to be home.

For the first few years, Daniel and his wife tried to please everyone and ended up exhausted, travelling across the country in one day. Eventually they agreed to alternate Christmas and invite both sides for a meal when it was their turn to host. Some relatives grumbled, but most adapted once they realised the couple weren’t choosing one family over the other. Daniel’s mother still occasionally hinted that he was neglecting his side, but he learnt not to treat every disappointed comment as a problem he had to fix. In-laws get along best when people talk honestly, share the effort and accept that marriage creates new relationships—not a competition for loyalty.
