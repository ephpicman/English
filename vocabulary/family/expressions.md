# Everyday Family Expressions

Practical expressions connected with family, parenting, relationships, and home life that are useful for understanding contemporary British English. The focus is on things you may hear in everyday conversation, at work, in messages, or in communication with a child's school—not rare literary language.

This file complements `idioms.md` and `proverbs.md`. It focuses on common indirect, figurative, and culturally meaningful expressions. Some entries are idiomatic expressions; others are euphemisms or common ways of framing family situations. They are included because understanding them helps you follow real conversations.

**Labels**
- **Priority:** High = worth learning actively; Useful = especially helpful for understanding others.
- **Register:** informal, neutral, semi-formal, or formal.
- **British English:** examples and usage notes prioritise contemporary UK English where relevant.
- CEFR levels are approximate learning guidance, not official classifications.

## High priority: everyday family and relationship talk

### 1. start a family
- **Meaning:** To have a child or begin having children.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in conversations about relationships, future plans, and life choices.
- **Example:** We're thinking about starting a family in the next couple of years.
- **Nuance:** Usually means having children, not simply getting married. It can be a sensitive topic; don't assume everyone wants or can have children.

### 2. settle down
- **Meaning:** To establish a stable life, often by committing to a partner, getting married, or having children.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Use:** Common in conversations about dating, adulthood, and future plans.
- **Example:** Everyone keeps asking him when he's going to settle down.
- **Nuance:** Can sound as if marriage and children are expected milestones. Depending on tone, it may be teasing or intrusive.

### 3. have a baby / be expecting
- **Meaning:** “Have a baby” can refer to giving birth or becoming a parent; “be expecting” is a common indirect way to say someone is pregnant.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** “We're expecting” is common when announcing a pregnancy.
- **Example:** They told us they're expecting their first baby in November.
- **Nuance:** Pregnancy can be private or sensitive. Let the person choose when and how to share the news.

### 4. due in [month] / due any day now
- **Meaning:** The expected time for a baby to be born.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common in pregnancy-related conversation.
- **Example:** She's due in early March. / The baby's due any day now.
- **Nuance:** “Due” is also used for deadlines and expected events; the context makes the meaning clear.

### 5. be brought up by someone
- **Meaning:** To be cared for and raised as a child by someone.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when talking about childhood, parents, grandparents, or foster carers.
- **Example:** I was brought up by my grandparents in Manchester.
- **Nuance:** “Brought up” is especially common in British English; “raised” is also widely used.

### 6. grow up in a [type of] family
- **Meaning:** To spend your childhood in a particular family situation or environment.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in personal conversations, school discussions, and writing about childhood.
- **Example:** She grew up in a large family with four brothers.
- **Nuance:** “Grow up in a single-parent family / a blended family / a close-knit family” are common combinations.

### 7. a close-knit family
- **Meaning:** A family whose members have strong relationships and support one another.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Common in conversation, personal profiles, and school or social contexts.
- **Example:** He's from a close-knit family, so they all get together most weekends.
- **Nuance:** Positive description; it doesn't necessarily mean relatives live together or never disagree.

### 8. a blended family
- **Meaning:** A family in which partners bring children from previous relationships into a new family unit.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in discussions of family structure, parenting, and schools.
- **Example:** It took a while for everyone to adjust to life in a blended family.
- **Nuance:** A neutral term. “Stepfamily” is also used, though the terms can have slightly different emphases.

### 9. a single-parent family / a single mum / a single dad
- **Meaning:** A family in which one parent is raising a child; “single mum/dad” is the everyday personal term.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral; “single mum/dad” is conversational.
- **Use:** Common in school forms, conversations, and everyday descriptions.
- **Example:** She's a single mum and works full-time.
- **Nuance:** Avoid assuming why someone is parenting alone or that they lack support.

### 10. the school run
- **Meaning:** The trip to take children to school or collect them, usually by car or on foot.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; especially British English.
- **Use:** Very common among parents and in everyday scheduling.
- **Example:** I can't make a meeting at 3 because I have to do the school run.
- **Nuance:** “Do the school run” is a common UK expression. It can refer to taking children to school, picking them up, or both.

### 11. the school pick-up / school drop-off
- **Meaning:** Collecting a child from school / taking a child to school.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in messages between parents and in school-related arrangements.
- **Example:** Can you do pick-up today? I've got a work call that might run late.
- **Nuance:** In the UK, “pick-up” and “drop-off” are widely understood; local school terminology may vary.

### 12. after-school club
- **Meaning:** An organised activity or childcare session for children after the school day ends.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral; British English.
- **Use:** Common in school communications and parent conversations.
- **Example:** I've booked him into after-school club on Tuesdays.
- **Nuance:** Often used without an article when referring to the service generally, though usage varies.

### 13. wraparound care
- **Meaning:** Childcare before and/or after normal school hours.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal; common in UK school contexts.
- **Use:** Likely to appear in school emails, policies, forms, and conversations about childcare.
- **Example:** Does the school offer wraparound care from 7.30 in the morning?
- **Nuance:** More common in school and childcare administration than casual conversation.

### 14. a school place
- **Meaning:** A place offered to a child at a school.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal; British English.
- **Use:** Common when discussing applications, admissions, and school allocation.
- **Example:** We found out yesterday that she's been offered a place at our first-choice school.
- **Nuance:** “Get a place” means receive an offer/admission; it does not necessarily mean the child has started attending.

### 15. be on the waiting list
- **Meaning:** To be waiting for an available place or appointment.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common for nurseries, schools, clubs, and healthcare appointments.
- **Example:** He's on the waiting list for the nursery.
- **Nuance:** This is not exclusively a family expression, but is very useful in family administration.

## High priority: everyday indirect expressions and family situations

### 16. expecting a little one
- **Meaning:** Expecting a baby; “little one” is an affectionate way to refer to a young child or baby.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; warm.
- **Use:** Common in friendly conversation and messages.
- **Example:** I heard you're expecting a little one—congratulations!
- **Nuance:** Friendly, but only say it when the pregnancy is known and the person is comfortable discussing it.

### 17. the little one(s)
- **Meaning:** An affectionate, informal way to refer to a young child or children.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common among parents, relatives, and people talking about childcare.
- **Example:** I'll need to leave by five to pick up the little ones.
- **Nuance:** Not limited to babies; often used for children generally.

### 18. be a handful
- **Meaning:** To be difficult to manage because someone is very energetic, demanding, or troublesome.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common when talking about children, but can describe adults or situations too.
- **Example:** The twins are a real handful at the moment, but they're lovely kids.
- **Nuance:** Often affectionate or mildly exasperated, not necessarily a serious criticism.

### 19. go through a phase
- **Meaning:** To behave in a particular way for a period of time, often as part of growing up.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common when discussing children's changing interests, moods, or behaviour.
- **Example:** He's going through a phase of refusing to wear anything but football shirts.
- **Nuance:** Often implies the behaviour is temporary, though that is not guaranteed.

### 20. push the boundaries
- **Meaning:** To test limits or rules to see what is allowed.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in parenting, education, and workplace contexts.
- **Example:** She's been pushing the boundaries lately to see how much screen time she'll get.
- **Nuance:** Can be a normal part of growing up; the phrase doesn't automatically imply serious misbehaviour.

### 21. act up
- **Meaning:** To behave badly or unusually; often used about children who are being disruptive.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common in everyday speech and parent–teacher conversations.
- **Example:** He was acting up in class yesterday, so the teacher sent me a message.
- **Nuance:** Also used for machines or health problems (“my back's acting up”), so context matters.

### 22. play up
- **Meaning:** In British English, to behave badly or be difficult; also used when a problem or device causes trouble.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially British English.
- **Use:** Common in family conversation.
- **Example:** The kids were playing up a bit because they were overtired.
- **Nuance:** “My knee's playing up” means the knee is causing problems. Meaning depends on the subject.

### 23. have a lot on
- **Meaning:** To have many things to do or worry about.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; common in British English.
- **Use:** Common when arranging family or work schedules.
- **Example:** I can't help at the school fair this week; we've got a lot on.
- **Nuance:** A tactful way to say you're busy without listing all your responsibilities.

### 24. juggle work and family
- **Meaning:** To manage work responsibilities and family responsibilities at the same time.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in workplace conversations, articles, and discussions about parenting.
- **Example:** She's trying to juggle work and family while her partner is away.
- **Nuance:** “Juggle” is metaphorical: it suggests competing demands that require careful management.

### 25. take time off for family reasons
- **Meaning:** To be absent from work for a period because of a family matter.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Useful at work when you want to give a brief explanation without sharing private details.
- **Example:** I need to take tomorrow morning off for family reasons.
- **Nuance:** A polite, privacy-preserving phrase; the exact leave category depends on the employer and situation.

### 26. have childcare sorted
- **Meaning:** To have made the necessary arrangements for looking after a child.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially British English.
- **Use:** Common when arranging work, appointments, or social plans.
- **Example:** I can stay late if I've got childcare sorted.
- **Nuance:** “Sorted” means arranged or dealt with; it is common British conversational English.

### 27. family commitments
- **Meaning:** Responsibilities or plans involving family that affect one's availability.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Common in workplace messages and scheduling.
- **Example:** I won't be able to attend the evening event because of family commitments.
- **Nuance:** A tactful phrase that does not require disclosing private details. It can refer to responsibilities or pre-existing plans.

### 28. keep someone in the loop
- **Meaning:** To make sure someone receives relevant updates and information.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common at work and in parent groups or school-related coordination.
- **Example:** Please keep me in the loop about any changes to the school trip.
- **Nuance:** Not family-specific, but highly practical for coordinating family matters.

### 29. touch base with someone
- **Meaning:** To contact someone briefly to check in or exchange updates.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Informal to semi-formal; common in work settings.
- **Use:** Useful with teachers, co-parents, relatives, or colleagues when a brief follow-up is needed.
- **Example:** I'll touch base with the class teacher after parents' evening.
- **Nuance:** Business jargon to some speakers; “check in with” is often simpler and more natural.

### 30. get in touch with the school
- **Meaning:** To contact the school.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in school communication and everyday family administration.
- **Example:** I'll get in touch with the school to let them know she'll be absent.
- **Nuance:** A natural, broadly useful alternative to the more formal “contact the school”.

## Useful expressions for sensitive family topics

### 31. have another one
- **Meaning:** To have another child.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common in casual conversation, but often asked as a personal question.
- **Example:** People keep asking us whether we're going to have another one.
- **Nuance:** The question can feel intrusive. Avoid asking unless you know the person is happy to discuss family planning.

### 32. be trying for a baby
- **Meaning:** To be actively trying to become pregnant or conceive a child with a partner.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Heard in personal conversations, but the subject is private.
- **Example:** They're trying for a baby, but they haven't told many people.
- **Nuance:** Don't ask people whether they are trying for a baby; fertility and pregnancy can be sensitive.

### 33. be an only child
- **Meaning:** To have no siblings.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in conversations about childhood and family size.
- **Example:** I was an only child, so I had no brothers or sisters to share a room with.
- **Nuance:** It describes family structure and does not imply that someone was lonely or spoiled.

### 34. be an empty nester
- **Meaning:** To be a parent whose children have grown up and left home.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Neutral; common in lifestyle and personal conversation.
- **Use:** Used when discussing a new stage of life after children move out.
- **Example:** Now that both children have moved out, we're getting used to being empty nesters.
- **Nuance:** “Empty nest” is the related noun phrase. It can be positive, sad, or mixed depending on the person.

### 35. move back in with your parents
- **Meaning:** To return to living in your parents' home, often after living independently.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in discussions of housing costs, relationships, study, or changing circumstances.
- **Example:** After university, he moved back in with his parents to save money.
- **Nuance:** It is not automatically a sign of failure; reasons vary widely.

### 36. be estranged from someone
- **Meaning:** To no longer have a close or active relationship with a relative or someone formerly close, often because of conflict.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2–C1
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Common in news, personal stories, and conversations about difficult family relationships.
- **Example:** She's been estranged from her father for years.
- **Nuance:** More formal than “not speaking to”. It does not tell you who is responsible or why the relationship ended.

### 37. be on speaking terms
- **Meaning:** To be willing to speak to someone, especially after an argument or falling-out.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when discussing strained relationships.
- **Example:** They had a big argument, and now they're barely on speaking terms.
- **Nuance:** “Barely on speaking terms” means they speak very little or only when necessary.

### 38. have a falling-out
- **Meaning:** To have an argument or disagreement that damages a relationship.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common for friends, siblings, and other relatives.
- **Example:** The sisters had a falling-out over their parents' house.
- **Nuance:** A falling-out can be temporary or long-lasting; the phrase itself does not tell you whether they reconciled.

### 39. patch things up
- **Meaning:** To restore a relationship after an argument or disagreement.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common in everyday conversations about family and friends.
- **Example:** They've finally patched things up after months of not speaking.
- **Nuance:** Usually suggests improving the relationship, not necessarily resolving every underlying issue.

### 40. make up with someone
- **Meaning:** To become friendly again after an argument.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common for children, siblings, partners, and friends.
- **Example:** Have you made up with your sister yet?
- **Nuance:** “Make up” has several meanings; “make up with someone” means reconcile.

### 41. fall out with someone
- **Meaning:** To have an argument and become unfriendly with someone.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Use:** Very common when talking about relatives or friends.
- **Example:** I fell out with my brother over something silly, but we're fine now.
- **Nuance:** In British English, “fall out with” is especially natural. American English often uses “have a falling-out with”.

### 42. not see eye to eye
- **Meaning:** To disagree with someone.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when describing differences of opinion between parents, children, partners, or relatives.
- **Example:** My dad and I don't always see eye to eye on politics.
- **Nuance:** Disagreement does not necessarily mean the relationship is bad.

### 43. set boundaries
- **Meaning:** To make clear what behaviour you will accept and what limits you need in a relationship.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal; common in contemporary conversation.
- **Use:** Common in discussions of parenting, family conflict, wellbeing, and relationships.
- **Example:** We've had to set boundaries about how often relatives can drop by unannounced.
- **Nuance:** Often used in modern wellbeing and relationship language; it does not automatically mean cutting someone off.

### 44. cut someone off
- **Meaning:** To stop communicating with or supporting someone; the exact meaning depends on context.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Can describe ending contact with a relative or withdrawing financial support.
- **Example:** After years of arguments, she decided to cut her father off.
- **Nuance:** It can mean ending contact, stopping money, or interrupting someone while speaking. Context is essential.

### 45. be there for someone
- **Meaning:** To support someone emotionally or practically when they need help.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common in conversations about family support, illness, stress, and difficult times.
- **Example:** Whatever happens, I'll always be there for you.
- **Nuance:** Often warm and reassuring; it can mean listening, helping, or simply being available.

### 46. lean on someone
- **Meaning:** To rely on someone for emotional or practical support.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in conversations about difficult periods and family support.
- **Example:** When things got difficult, she leaned on her sister for support.
- **Nuance:** Figurative rather than physical; “lean on” can also mean pressure someone, so context matters.

### 47. take after someone
- **Meaning:** To resemble a relative in appearance, personality, or behaviour.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common when talking about children and parents.
- **Example:** He takes after his mum—he's just as chatty.
- **Nuance:** Already covered in `idioms.md`; repeated here only as a reminder of a high-frequency expression. See that file for fuller notes.

### 48. look like someone / look just like someone
- **Meaning:** To resemble someone physically.
- **Priority / level:** High · A1–A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common in family conversations and when looking at photographs.
- **Example:** Your little boy looks just like you.
- **Nuance:** This is a normal expression rather than an idiom, but it is essential everyday language and helps interpret comments about family resemblance.

## School and parenting communication

### 49. keep me posted
- **Meaning:** Continue giving me updates as things change.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common in messages with teachers, other parents, relatives, and colleagues.
- **Example:** Please keep me posted if his symptoms get worse or he needs to be collected.
- **Nuance:** Friendly and natural; in a more formal email, “Please keep me informed” may be preferable.

### 50. just a heads-up
- **Meaning:** A brief advance warning or piece of useful information.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Common in parent group chats, school messages, and workplace communication.
- **Example:** Just a heads-up: the school gates will be closed on Friday.
- **Nuance:** Friendly and efficient, but informal. For a formal school email, use “Please note that…”

### 51. run late
- **Meaning:** To be later than planned.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Extremely common when coordinating school pick-ups, childcare, and family plans.
- **Example:** I'm running late—could you wait with her for five minutes?
- **Nuance:** “I'm running late” is natural; “I'm late-running” is not.

### 52. be held up
- **Meaning:** To be delayed, often by traffic, work, or an unexpected problem.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when explaining delays to family members or school staff.
- **Example:** I'm held up in traffic, so I'll be ten minutes late for pick-up.
- **Nuance:** Often implies that the delay is not intentional.

### 53. pick someone up
- **Meaning:** To collect someone from a place, especially a child from school or an activity.
- **Priority / level:** High · A1–A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Essential for family logistics.
- **Example:** Could you pick the kids up from football practice?
- **Nuance:** The phrasal verb has other meanings too, but this is a very common family use.

### 54. drop someone off
- **Meaning:** To take someone somewhere and leave them there.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common for school, nursery, clubs, and appointments.
- **Example:** I'll drop her off at school on my way to work.
- **Nuance:** Often paired with “pick up”: “I'll drop him off, and you can pick him up.”

### 55. settle in
- **Meaning:** To become comfortable in a new place, routine, school, or situation.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common when a child starts a new school, nursery, or class.
- **Example:** How's your daughter settling in at her new school?
- **Nuance:** A very natural question to ask a parent after a change.

### 56. have a good day at school?
- **Meaning:** A common question about a child's experience at school.
- **Priority / level:** High · A1–A2
- **Register:** Neutral, conversational.
- **Use:** Everyday parent–child conversation. Often phrased “Did you have a good day at school?”
- **Example:** Did you have a good day at school, sweetheart?
- **Nuance:** A practical conversational phrase rather than an idiom; included because everyday fluency matters more than strict category boundaries.

### 57. parent-teacher meeting / parents' evening
- **Meaning:** A scheduled meeting between a child's parent(s) and teacher(s) to discuss progress and wellbeing.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral; “parents' evening” is common in the UK.
- **Use:** Common school vocabulary.
- **Example:** I've got parents' evening at six, so I'll be home a bit later.
- **Nuance:** Exact names vary by school and country.

### 58. be doing well at school
- **Meaning:** To be making good progress or performing well at school.
- **Priority / level:** High · A2–B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in school reports and conversations with teachers.
- **Example:** His teacher says he's doing well at school, especially in reading.
- **Nuance:** Can refer to academic progress, behaviour, or general adjustment depending on context.

## Euphemisms and indirect language worth recognising

### 59. pass away
- **Meaning:** A gentle, indirect way to say “die”.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral to formal; considerate.
- **Use:** Common in conversations, messages, and announcements about bereavement.
- **Example:** Her grandfather passed away last winter.
- **Nuance:** Softer than “die”; direct wording may be preferred in medical or factual contexts.

### 60. lose someone
- **Meaning:** A common indirect way to say that someone has died.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral, sensitive.
- **Use:** Common when discussing bereavement.
- **Example:** I'm so sorry to hear you've lost your mum.
- **Nuance:** Context distinguishes death from other meanings of “lose”. Be gentle and avoid assuming details.

### 61. be expecting
- **Meaning:** To be pregnant, expressed indirectly.
- **Priority / level:** High · B1
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in pregnancy announcements and everyday conversation.
- **Example:** They're expecting, but they're waiting a little longer before telling everyone.
- **Nuance:** Also appears in entry 3; included here to highlight its indirect meaning. No need to learn it twice.

### 62. no longer with us
- **Meaning:** A euphemism meaning that someone has died.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Gentle, somewhat formal or ceremonial.
- **Use:** Heard in obituaries, memorials, and careful conversation, though it can sound euphemistic.
- **Example:** Her parents are no longer with us, but her aunt lives nearby.
- **Nuance:** Less direct and sometimes old-fashioned; “passed away” is often more natural in everyday conversation.

### 63. having a bit of a wobble
- **Meaning:** Feeling temporarily upset, anxious, uncertain, or overwhelmed.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Informal; British English.
- **Use:** Common conversational understatement, including when a child is struggling with a change or an adult feels overwhelmed.
- **Example:** He had a bit of a wobble on his first morning at nursery, but he settled quickly.
- **Nuance:** Mild, often affectionate understatement; not usually used for a serious mental-health crisis.

### 64. going through a rough patch
- **Meaning:** Experiencing a difficult period, often in a relationship or family life.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Use:** Common in everyday conversation about relationships, finances, or stress.
- **Example:** They're going through a rough patch, but they're trying to work things out.
- **Nuance:** Does not necessarily mean the relationship is ending.

### 65. things are a bit up in the air
- **Meaning:** Plans or circumstances are uncertain and not yet settled.
- **Priority / level:** High · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common when discussing childcare, moving house, school arrangements, or family plans.
- **Example:** Our childcare arrangements are a bit up in the air for next week.
- **Nuance:** Useful for communicating uncertainty without overexplaining.

### 66. it's been a lot
- **Meaning:** A conversational understatement meaning that a situation has been emotionally or practically demanding.
- **Priority / level:** Useful · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; contemporary conversational English.
- **Use:** Common when someone has been dealing with stress, a new baby, illness, bereavement, or family changes.
- **Example:** The last few weeks have been a lot, to be honest.
- **Nuance:** Often invites empathy rather than advice. It is vague by design; the speaker may not want to share details.

## What to learn first

1. Learn the **High priority** entries first, especially those used in school logistics and everyday conversation.
2. Practise common chunks as whole units: “do the school run”, “pick someone up”, “keep me posted”, “go through a phase”, “fall out with someone”, and “patch things up”.
3. Recognise euphemisms like “passed away” and “no longer with us”, but choose direct language when clarity matters.
