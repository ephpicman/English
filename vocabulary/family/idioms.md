# Family Idioms

Idioms connected with family relationships, parenthood, marriage, inheritance, and family life. Only established idiomatic expressions are included here; proverbs, sayings, ordinary collocations, and transparent phrasal verbs are excluded.

**How to read the labels**
- **CEFR level:** an approximate learning priority, not an official or universally agreed CEFR classification. Levels can vary by context and dictionary.
- **Register:** **formal**, **semi-formal**, **neutral**, or **informal**.
- **Use note:** explains when the idiom sounds natural and any important connotation or limitation.

---

## A1–A2: high-priority everyday idioms

### 1. run in the family
- **Meaning:** To be a quality, ability, habit, or condition shared by several members of a family.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in everyday conversation. Often used for looks, talents, habits, and inherited traits; it does not necessarily mean a trait is genetically inherited.
- **Example:** Musical talent seems to **run in the family**; both of her children play the piano.
- **Caution:** This is about a shared family trait, not simply about people spending time together.

### 2. take after someone
- **Meaning:** To resemble an older relative in appearance, behaviour, or personality.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Very common in conversation, especially when talking about children and parents or grandparents.
- **Example:** Mia really **takes after her mother**—they have the same laugh.
- **Caution:** This is a fixed idiomatic phrasal expression, but it is not a substitute for every meaning of “look like”; it can refer to personality as well as appearance.

### 3. the black sheep (of the family)
- **Meaning:** A family member regarded as different from the others, especially because their behaviour is disapproved of.
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Use:** Use when describing a family member seen as an outsider or as bringing embarrassment to the family.
- **Example:** He was considered **the black sheep of the family** because he rejected the career his relatives expected him to follow.
- **Caution:** It can sound judgemental or hurtful. Avoid applying it casually to someone in a sensitive conversation.

### 4. flesh and blood
- **Meaning:** A person's family or relatives, especially when emphasising a strong natural connection.
- **Register:** Neutral; can sound emotional or emphatic.
- **Use:** Often appears in “one's own flesh and blood”. It may express loyalty, obligation, or disbelief about how a relative behaves.
- **Example:** She could not imagine abandoning her **own flesh and blood**.
- **Caution:** It can imply that family connection should matter more than it actually does to the speaker; it is not always affectionate.

### 5. be the apple of someone's eye
- **Meaning:** To be someone who is loved and cherished very much.
- **Register:** Neutral, slightly traditional or affectionate.
- **Use:** Often used by or about parents, grandparents, and children; it can also describe someone outside the family.
- **Example:** His granddaughter is **the apple of his eye**.
- **Caution:** Warm and affectionate, but somewhat expressive; it may sound sentimental in formal or factual writing.

## B1–B2: useful idioms for relationships and family life

### 6. tie the knot
- **Meaning:** To get married.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common in conversation and light writing about a wedding or marriage.
- **Example:** After dating for six years, they finally **tied the knot** in a small ceremony.
- **Caution:** It is friendly and conversational; in legal, official, or highly formal writing, use “get married” or “marry”.

### 7. get on like a house on fire
- **Meaning:** To become friendly very quickly and get along extremely well.
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Use:** Suitable for describing relatives, in-laws, friends, or people who have just met.
- **Example:** I was nervous about meeting my new sister-in-law, but we **got on like a house on fire**.
- **Caution:** This describes a very strong, quick rapport, not merely a polite relationship.

### 8. wear the trousers
- **Meaning:** To be the person who has the most control or authority in a relationship or household.
- **Register:** Informal; especially British English.
- **Use:** Usually said about a couple or household. It can be humorous, critical, or based on traditional gender stereotypes.
- **Example:** Everyone assumes her husband makes the decisions, but she is the one who **wears the trousers** at home.
- **Caution:** Potentially old-fashioned or sexist in implication. Avoid it in sensitive professional discussions about relationships.

### 9. have someone wrapped around your little finger
- **Meaning:** To be able to persuade or control someone easily, often because they are very fond of you.
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Use:** Frequently used about children who can easily persuade parents or grandparents, but also applies to adults.
- **Example:** Their youngest son **has his grandparents wrapped around his little finger**.
- **Caution:** Often playful, but it can suggest manipulation if the context is negative.

### 10. a chip off the old block
- **Meaning:** Someone, often a child, who strongly resembles one of their parents in character or behaviour.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; slightly old-fashioned.
- **Use:** Often said when a child displays a parent's familiar manner, talent, or temperament.
- **Example:** Daniel is **a chip off the old block**—he has the same patience and practical skills as his father.
- **Caution:** It can suggest either a positive or negative resemblance, depending on context.

### 11. like a family
- **Meaning:** In a close, supportive way that resembles the relationships within a family, even when the people are not related.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Commonly used with verbs such as “feel” or “treat”: “feel like a family”, “treat someone like family”. The expression is idiomatic in context, but the exact wording varies.
- **Example:** After years of working together, the team **feels like a family**.
- **Caution:** This is less fixed than the other entries; use it only when the context genuinely suggests closeness and mutual support.

### 12. keep it in the family
- **Meaning:** To keep something, such as information, a business, or a responsibility, within the family rather than involving outsiders.
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Use:** The exact implication depends on context; it can refer to a family business, a private matter, or passing something on to relatives.
- **Example:** They decided to **keep the business in the family** and pass it on to their daughter.
- **Caution:** It can imply secrecy or exclusion when used about information or decisions.

### 13. a family affair
- **Meaning:** An event, activity, or matter involving mainly family members.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in conversation and journalism, for example about a small wedding, celebration, or family-run activity.
- **Example:** They kept the wedding **a family affair**, inviting only close relatives.
- **Caution:** This is a set idiomatic expression, but it is not exclusively about positive events; context determines the tone.

### 14. skeletons in the closet
- **Meaning:** Secrets from someone's past, often embarrassing or shameful, that they do not want others to discover.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; the British English variant is often “skeletons in the cupboard”.
- **Use:** Can refer to family secrets or to a person's private past. Use when the idea of concealed, potentially damaging information matters.
- **Example:** While researching their family history, they discovered a few **skeletons in the cupboard**.
- **Caution:** It suggests potentially troubling secrets, not simply private information. “Closet” is more common in American English; “cupboard” is common in British English.

### 15. be in someone's blood
- **Meaning:** To be a natural or deeply ingrained part of someone's character, interests, or family tradition.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Often used for a skill, passion, or long-standing family activity.
- **Example:** Cooking is **in her blood**; her parents and grandparents all worked as chefs.
- **Caution:** Usually figurative, not a literal medical statement. It may imply inheritance, but does not prove a genetic cause.

### 16. be born with a silver spoon in your mouth
- **Meaning:** To be born into a wealthy or privileged family.
- **Register:** Neutral to informal, often critical.
- **Use:** Used when discussing inherited wealth, privilege, or advantages someone had from birth.
- **Example:** He was **born with a silver spoon in his mouth**, so he never had to worry about paying university fees.
- **Caution:** Often carries criticism or resentment; avoid it if you want to sound completely neutral.

## C1–C2: nuanced and less everyday idioms

### 17. be the spitting image of someone
- **Meaning:** To look extremely similar to another person, especially a parent or other relative.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; common in British English.
- **Use:** Most often used for physical resemblance across generations.
- **Example:** At the same age, she was **the spitting image of her mother**.
- **Caution:** Primarily about appearance, not personality. “A dead ringer for someone” is a related informal idiom, but is not included as a separate entry here.

### 18. be cut from the same cloth
- **Meaning:** To be very similar in character, attitudes, or behaviour.
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Can describe relatives who share a temperament or values, but also people who are not related.
- **Example:** The two brothers are **cut from the same cloth**: both are cautious, private, and fiercely independent.
- **Caution:** It can be approving or critical depending on the qualities being described.

### 19. be a chip off the old block
- **Meaning:** To resemble a parent in personality or behaviour.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Common enough to recognise, but somewhat traditional; see also the B1–B2 entry above.
- **Example:** Her son is **a chip off the old block**, especially when it comes to his stubbornness.
- **Caution:** This duplicates the earlier expression intentionally only as a reminder of its nuance; learn it once rather than as a separate idiom.

### 20. blood relative
- **Meaning:** A person related to someone by birth rather than only by marriage or another social relationship.
- **Register:** Neutral to formal.
- **Use:** Common in legal, medical, genealogical, and everyday contexts. However, this is a literal compound term rather than a true idiom.
- **Example:** The form asks whether the donor is a **blood relative**.
- **Caution:** **Not an idiom.** It is included here as a boundary example only and should not be learned as part of the idiom list.

---

## Important exclusions

The following are deliberately **not** included as idioms:
- **“Blood is thicker than water”** — a proverb.
- **“Like father, like son”** and **“The apple doesn't fall far from the tree”** — proverbs/sayings.
- **“Take after someone”** is included above as a fixed idiomatic phrasal expression, but ordinary phrasal verbs such as “bring up a child” are not collected here.
- Ordinary collocations such as **close-knit family**, **family ties**, **single parent**, and **family gathering** are not idioms.
- **“A happy family”** and **“one big happy family”** are not treated as standalone idioms here.

**Study tip:** Learn the meaning, register, and example together. The CEFR labels above are approximate priorities rather than official level assignments, and some idioms are more common in British English than others.
