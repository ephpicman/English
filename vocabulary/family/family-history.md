# Family History

**generation**: `/ˌdʒenəˈreɪʃn/` - `n. [C]`  
All the people born or living around the same time, especially within a family or society.
  - Each generation in the family has lived in a different city.
  - The **younger generation** is more comfortable with technology.
  - There is a **generation gap** between them.
  - The tradition has been passed down **from generation to generation**.

**lineage**: `/ˈlɪniɪdʒ/` - `n. [C, U]`  
The line of people from whom a person is descended.
  - The historian traced her lineage back to the eighteenth century.
  - They traced their **family lineage** back to Wales.

**lineal**: `/ˈlɪniəl/` - `adj.`  
Related directly to someone's ancestors or descendants.
  - The inheritance passed to his lineal descendants.
  - She is a **lineal descendant** of the original owner.
  - The property passed to his **lineal heirs**.

**inheritance**: `/ɪnˈherɪtəns/` - `n. [C, U]`  
Money or property received from someone who has died, or the passing on of genetic characteristics.
  - She used her inheritance to buy a small flat.
  - He received a **large inheritance** from his aunt.
  - They are **disputing the inheritance**.

**inherited**: `/ɪnˈherɪtɪd/` - `adj.`  
Received from a parent or ancestor, especially through genes or inheritance.
  - He has inherited his father's blue eyes.
  - She has **inherited traits** from both parents.

**heir**: `/eə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A person who is legally entitled to inherit another person's money, property or title.
  - The prince was named the heir to the throne.
  - He is the **sole heir** to the estate.
  - The king named his son as **heir to the throne**.

**heiress**: `/ˈeərəs/` - `n. [C]`  
A woman who is legally entitled to inherit money, property or a title.
  - The heiress inherited the family estate.
  - She was the **sole heiress** to the family business.

**heirloom**: `/ˈeəluːm/` - `n. [C]`  
An object that has belonged to a family for many years and is passed from one generation to another.
  - The silver necklace is a family heirloom passed down for generations.
  - The ring is a **family heirloom**.
  - She treasured the **priceless heirloom**.

**legacy**: `/ˈleɡəsi/` - `n. [C]`  
Money or property left to someone in a will; more broadly, something handed down from the past.
  - Her grandmother left her a small legacy in her will.
  - He left a **lasting legacy** to the community.
  - The will included a **small legacy** for his niece.

**will**: `/wɪl/` - `n. [C]`  
A legal document stating what should happen to someone's money and property after their death.
  - He changed his will to include his grandchildren.
  - She made a **last will and testament**.

**estate**: `/ɪˈsteɪt/` - `n. [C]`  
All the money and property that someone owns, especially when they die.
  - The solicitor handled the estate after her death.
  - The solicitor dealt with the **deceased's estate**.
  - The house formed part of the **family estate**.

**biological**: `/ˌbaɪəˈlɒdʒɪkl/` - `adj.`  
Related to living organisms, or to a person's biological parents rather than adoptive parents.
  - They wanted to learn more about their biological relatives.
  - They are **biological relatives**.
  - The test established a **biological relationship**.

**genetic**: `/dʒəˈnetɪk/` - `adj.`  
Related to genes or inherited characteristics.
  - The doctor explained that the condition can be genetic.
  - They discussed their **genetic background**.

**heredity**: `/həˈredəti/` - `n. [U]`  
The passing of genetic characteristics from parents to children.
  - Heredity can influence a person's height and eye colour.
  - The role of **heredity and environment** is complex.
  - Eye colour is partly determined by **heredity**.

**inherit**: `/ɪnˈherɪt/` - `v. [T]`  
To receive money, property or a title from someone who has died, or to receive a genetic characteristic from a parent.
  - She will inherit the house when her aunt dies.

**hereditary**: `/həˈredɪtri/` - `adj.`  
Transmitted genetically from one generation to the next.
  - The doctor asked whether the illness was hereditary.
  - The doctor asked about **hereditary conditions**.
  - It is a **hereditary disease**.

**family resemblance**: `/ˌfæməli rɪˈzembləns/` - `n. [C, U]`  
Similarity in appearance or characteristics between people in the same family.
  - There is a strong family resemblance between the two sisters.
  - There is a **striking family resemblance** between them.
  - The children share a **strong family resemblance**.

**genealogy**: `/ˌdʒiːniˈælədʒi/` - `n. [U]`  
The study or tracing of family history and lines of descent.
  - He became interested in genealogy and began researching old records.
  - She has a keen interest in **family genealogy**.
  - He researched his **genealogy online**.

**genealogist**: `/ˌdʒiːniˈælədʒɪst/` - `n. [C]`  
A person who studies or traces family histories.
  - The genealogist found a marriage certificate from 1842.

**generational**: `/ˌdʒenəˈreɪʃənl/` - `adj.`  
Related to a generation or to differences between generations.
  - The book explores generational differences in attitudes to work.
  - The report highlights **generational differences**.
  - They studied **generational change** in family life.

**intergenerational**: `/ˌɪntədʒenəˈreɪʃənl/` - `adj.`  
Existing or occurring between different generations.
  - The project encourages intergenerational relationships between teenagers and older people.
  - The centre runs **intergenerational activities**.
  - They aim to promote **intergenerational understanding**.

**familial**: `/fəˈmɪliəl/` - `adj.`  
Occurring among or characteristic of members of the same family.
  - The researchers found a familial pattern in the condition.
  - The study found a **familial link** between the cases.
  - There is a **familial history** of the condition.

**family tree**: `/ˌfæməli ˈtriː/` - `n. [C]`  
A diagram showing the relationships between members of a family over several generations.
  - We added our new baby to the family tree.
  - We created a **family tree** for a school project.
  - She added the names to her **family tree**.

**lineal descendant**: `/ˌlɪniəl dɪˈsendənt/` - `n. [C]`  
A person who descends directly from someone, such as their child, grandchild or great-grandchild.
  - As a lineal descendant of the founder, she was eligible to inherit the property.
  - He is a **direct lineal descendant** of the founder.
  - The title can pass only to a **lineal descendant**.

**ancestor**: `/ˈænsestə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A person in your family who lived before you, usually several generations ago.
  - One of my ancestors moved to Scotland in the nineteenth century.
  - They honoured their **common ancestor**.
  - One **distant ancestor** came from Ireland.

**ancestry**: `/ˈænsestri/` - `n. [U]`  
A person's family origins or line of descent.
  - A DNA test gave him more information about his ancestry.
  - She researched her **Irish ancestry**.
  - The test revealed his **family ancestry**.

**descendant**: `/dɪˈsendənt/` - `n. [C]`  
A person who is descended from someone who lived before them.
  - She is a direct descendant of the village's first mayor.
  - He is a **direct descendant** of the poet.
  - The house is still owned by a **descendant of the family**.

**consanguinity**: `/ˌkɒnsæŋˈɡwɪnəti/` - `n. [U]`  
The relationship between people who are related by blood.
  - The law places restrictions on marriage between close relatives because of consanguinity.
  - The law prohibits marriage within certain **degrees of consanguinity**.
  - The study examined **consanguinity rates** in the region.

**consanguineous**: `/ˌkɒnsæŋˈɡwɪniəs/` - `adj.`  
Related by blood.
  - The study examined genetic risks in consanguineous couples.
  - The report discussed **consanguineous marriages**.
  - They studied health risks among **consanguineous couples**.

## Idioms

**run in the family**
- **Meaning:** To be a quality, ability, habit, or condition shared by several members of a family.
- Register: Neutral.
- **Use:** Common in everyday conversation. Often used for looks, talents, habits, and inherited traits; it does not necessarily mean a trait is genetically inherited.
- **Example:** Musical talent seems to **run in the family**; both of her children play the piano.
- **Caution:** This is about a shared family trait, not simply about people spending time together.

**a chip off the old block**
- **Meaning:** Someone, often a child, who strongly resembles one of their parents in character or behaviour.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; slightly old-fashioned.
- **Use:** Often said when a child displays a parent's familiar manner, talent, or temperament.
- **Example:** Daniel is **a chip off the old block**—he has the same patience and practical skills as his father.
- **Caution:** It can suggest either a positive or negative resemblance, depending on context.

**be in someone's blood**
- **Meaning:** To be a natural or deeply ingrained part of someone's character, interests, or family tradition.
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Use:** Often used for a skill, passion, or long-standing family activity.
- **Example:** Cooking is **in her blood**; her parents and grandparents all worked as chefs.
- **Caution:** Usually figurative, not a literal medical statement. It may imply inheritance, but does not prove a genetic cause.

## Proverbs

**Like father, like son.**
- **Meaning:** A son often resembles his father in behaviour, interests, or character. The expression is also sometimes adapted for other parent–child relationships.
- **Register:** Neutral; traditional.
- **Use:** Said when a child behaves or thinks like their parent. It can be affectionate, amused, or critical.
- **Example:** “He’s already fixing things around the house just like his dad.” “Like father, like son!”
- **Caution:** The traditional wording is gender-specific. Do not assume children must resemble their parents.

**Like mother, like daughter.**
- **Meaning:** A daughter resembles her mother in behaviour, character, or interests.
- **Register:** Neutral; traditional.
- **Use:** A gender-specific counterpart to “like father, like son”; it can be affectionate, amused, or critical.
- **Example:** Both are fearless on stage—like mother, like daughter.
- **Caution:** It can reinforce stereotypes or expectations about how daughters should behave. Use only when the resemblance is relevant and welcome.

**The apple doesn’t fall far from the tree.**
- **Meaning:** Children often resemble their parents in character, habits, or abilities.
- **Register:** Neutral; conversational.
- **Use:** Common when a child shows a trait also seen in a parent. The resemblance can be positive or negative.
- **Example:** Her daughter is just as determined as she is—the apple doesn’t fall far from the tree.
- **Caution:** This is a proverb, not just a literal comparison. It describes a perceived pattern, not a rule that applies to every family.

**The sins of the fathers are visited upon the children.**
- **Meaning:** The harmful actions or mistakes of one generation can have consequences for later generations.
- **Register:** Formal to literary; biblical in wording.
- **Use:** Used in serious discussion of family trauma, inherited disadvantage, or the long-term consequences of parents’ actions.
- **Example:** The novel explores how the sins of the fathers are visited upon the children, as one generation’s choices shape the next.
- **Caution:** It can sound moralising and may unfairly imply that children are responsible for their parents’ actions. The phrase has biblical roots and is often quoted rather than used casually.

**Blood will tell.**
- **Meaning:** A person’s family background or inherited traits are believed eventually to become apparent in their behaviour or abilities.
- **Register:** Traditional; neutral to critical depending on context.
- **Use:** Sometimes used when someone displays a trait associated with their relatives.
- **Example:** When the child showed the same remarkable musical ear as her grandfather, one relative said, “Blood will tell.”
- **Caution:** It can imply that character is determined by ancestry and may carry classist or hereditary assumptions. Do not treat it as scientific evidence.

## Phrasal Verbs

**take after someone**
- **Meaning:** To resemble a parent or another relative in appearance or personality.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** She's very patient—she takes after her mum.
- **Usage note:** Usually refers to family resemblance, especially personality or appearance.

## Practice: Dialogue, Monologue and Reading

### 1. Dialogue: Building a Family Tree

**Ella:** I'm putting together our **family tree**. Do you know who our earliest **ancestor** was?

**Noah:** Grandma has been researching our **ancestry** and **lineage**. She says our **family history** goes back several generations.

**Ella:** Is our **genealogy** complete?

**Noah:** Not yet. A **genealogist** helped us check old records, but we still need to confirm one **lineal descendant** in the records. We also found a **heirloom** that belonged to an **heir** named in an old **will**.

**Ella:** Was there an **inheritance**?

**Noah:** Yes, part of the **estate** was passed down. Some property was **inherited**, but the family's greatest **legacy** was the stories they left us.

**Ella:** Do we all have the same **family resemblance**?

**Noah:** We share some **genetic** traits, but **heredity** isn't the whole story. Our **familial** features are interesting, and we may have **hereditary** traits, but each person is different.

### 2. Monologue: What We Inherit

Researching my family has taught me that inheritance is more than money. Each **generation** leaves a **legacy**, whether through stories, values, possessions, or property. My **ancestors** form part of my **ancestry** and **lineage**, and our **family tree** helps us trace each **lineal descendant**. Some features show a **family resemblance** and may reflect **genetic** inheritance, **heredity**, or **hereditary** characteristics. Other things are cultural: traditions and memories passed down **intergenerationally**. Our **genealogy** project, supported by a professional **genealogist**, has revealed a complicated history, including **consanguinity**—a relationship between people who share an ancestor—and the technical adjective **consanguineous**. We have also learned about **inheritance**, an **heir** and an **heiress**, an old **will**, and the family **estate**. A treasured **heirloom** reminds us that what is **inherited** can carry emotional meaning. Some influences are **generational**, while others are **intergenerational** and connect people across several age groups.

### 3. Reading: Records, Relationships and Inheritance

**Family history** explores the people and events that shaped a family. A **family tree** organises relationships, while **genealogy** is the study of family descent; a **genealogist** researches records to establish **ancestry** and **lineage**. An **ancestor** is someone from whom a person is descended, and a **descendant** is someone who comes later in the line. A **lineal descendant** is connected through a direct line of descent. **Lineal** describes that direct relationship. The word **generation** refers to people born around the same period, and **generational** describes something relating to a particular generation; **intergenerational** describes connections or effects between generations. **Familial** means related to a family. **Biological** and **genetic** describe relationships or characteristics connected with living organisms and genes. **Heredity** is the passing of characteristics from parents to children; **hereditary** means passed down genetically. A **family resemblance** is a similarity in appearance or manner among relatives. **Consanguinity** means a blood relationship, and **consanguineous** describes people related by blood. Property and possessions may also pass between generations: **inheritance** is what is received, to **inherit** is to receive it, and something **inherited** has been received from someone else. An **heir** or **heiress** may be entitled to inherit under a **will**, a legal document describing how a person's **estate** should be distributed. An **heirloom** is a valued object passed down through a family, while a **legacy** can be property, influence, or something lasting left behind.
