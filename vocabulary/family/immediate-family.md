# Immediate Family

## Parents

**parent**: `/ˈpeərənt/` - `n. [C]`  
A person's mother or father.
  - Each parent was invited to the school meeting.
  - Both parents attended the **parent-teacher meeting**.

**mother**: `/ˈmʌðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A female parent.
  - His mother works at the local hospital.
  - She is a **single mother** of two.

**father**: `/ˈfɑːðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A male parent.
  - Her father taught her to swim.
  - He is a **proud father** of three.

**mum**: `/mʌm/` - `n. [C]`  
An informal British word for mother.
  - Mum is picking me up after school.

**dad**: `/dæd/` - `n. [C]`  
An informal word for father.
  - Dad made pancakes for breakfast.

**mummy**: `/ˈmʌmi/` - `n. [C]`  
An informal word for mother, often used by children.
  - The toddler called for her mummy when she woke up.
  - The baby called for **Mummy and Daddy**.
  - She drew a picture for **Mummy**.

**daddy**: `/ˈdædi/` - `n. [C]`  
An informal word for father, often used by children.
  - The little boy ran to his daddy at the gate.
  - The little girl held **Daddy's hand**.
  - He shouted for **Daddy** from his bedroom.

**step-parent**: `/ˈstep peərənt/` - `n. [C]`  
The husband or wife of your mother or father who is not your biological parent.
  - Her step-parent helped her settle into the new school.
  - The school invited all **parents and step-parents**.

**stepmother**: `/ˈstepmʌðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
The wife of your father who is not your biological mother.
  - His stepmother welcomed him into her home.
  - She went on holiday with her **stepmother and father**.

**stepfather**: `/ˈstepfɑːðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
The husband of your mother who is not your biological father.
  - Her stepfather taught her how to drive.
  - She lives with her mother and **stepfather**.

## Children

**child**: `/tʃaɪld/` - `n. [C]`  
A son or daughter of any age in relation to their parents.
  - Their only child is now at university.
  - They have one **young child**.
  - The school provides **child care** for working parents.

**children**: `/ˈtʃɪldrən/` - `n. [pl.]`  
The plural of child; more than one son or daughter.
  - The children played in the garden after lunch.
  - The **young children** played outside.
  - They are raising **three children**.

**son**: `/sʌn/` - `n. [C]`  
A person's male child.
  - Their son lives and works in Manchester.
  - Their **eldest son** lives abroad.
  - She is the **mother of a son**.

**daughter**: `/ˈdɔːtə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A person's female child.
  - Her daughter visits her every weekend.
  - Their **youngest daughter** starts university soon.
  - He has a **daughter and two sons**.

**stepchild**: `/ˈsteptʃaɪld/` - `n. [C]`  
The child of your husband or wife from an earlier relationship.
  - He treats his stepchild with the same care as his own children.
  - He has a good relationship with his **stepchild**.

**stepson**: `/ˈstepsʌn/` - `n. [C]`  
The son of your husband or wife from an earlier relationship.
  - Her stepson came to stay during the summer holidays.
  - He has become close to his **stepson**.

**stepdaughter**: `/ˈstepdɔːtə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
The daughter of your husband or wife from an earlier relationship.
  - He helped his stepdaughter prepare for her exams.
  - She helped her **stepdaughter with homework**.

**offspring**: `/ˈɒfsprɪŋ/` - `n. [C, U]`  
A person's child or children.
  - The bird protects its offspring from predators.
  - Many animals protect their **young offspring**.
  - The parents care for their **offspring** until they can survive alone.

**progeny**: `/ˈprɒdʒəni/` - `n. [U]`  
A person's children or descendants; a formal word.
  - The king hoped his progeny would continue the family line.
  - The king hoped to leave **male progeny**.
  - The plant produces **numerous progeny**.

**only child**: `/ˌəʊnli ˈtʃaɪld/` - `n. [C]`  
A child who has no brothers or sisters.
  - As an only child, she never had to share a bedroom with a sibling.
  - As an **only child**, he had his own bedroom.
  - She was an **only child in the family**.

**twin**: `/twɪn/` - `n. [C]`  
One of two children born to the same mother at the same time.
  - My twin was born ten minutes before me.
  - They are **identical twins**.
  - She gave birth to **twins** last night.

**identical twin**: `/aɪˌdentɪkl ˈtwɪn/` - `n. [C]`  
One of two twins that developed from the same fertilised egg and are genetically almost identical.
  - Although she is an identical twin, I can tell her apart from her sister.
  - She is an **identical twin sister**.

**non-identical twin**: `/ˌnɒn aɪˌdentɪkl ˈtwɪn/` - `n. [C]`  
One of two twins that developed from separate eggs and are not genetically identical.
  - The non-identical twins have different eye colours and look quite different.

**triplet**: `/ˈtrɪplət/` - `n. [C]`  
One of three children born to the same mother at the same time.
  - One triplet was born slightly smaller than the other two.

**quadruplet**: `/kwɒˈdruːplət/` - `n. [C]`  
One of four children born to the same mother at the same time.
  - The quadruplet was one of four babies born that morning.

**multiple**: `/ˈmʌltɪpl/` - `n. [C, usually plural]`  
One of a set of two or more babies born to the same mother at the same time; this meaning is usually used in the plural, multiples.
  - The hospital has a specialist team to support parents of multiples.
  - The clinic specialises in **multiple births**.

## Siblings

**sibling**: `/ˈsɪblɪŋ/` - `n. [C]`  
A brother or sister.
  - Each sibling received a copy of the photograph.
  - She has two **older siblings**.

**brother**: `/ˈbrʌðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A male sibling.
  - Her brother works in a bookshop.
  - Her **older brother** helped her.
  - He has a **younger brother**.

**sister**: `/ˈsɪstə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A female sibling.
  - My sister lives in the flat upstairs.
  - My **elder sister** lives nearby.
  - She is his **younger sister**.

**half-brother**: `/ˈhɑːf brʌðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A brother with whom you share one biological parent.
  - He and his half-brother share the same mother but have different fathers.
  - He grew up with his **half-brother**.
  - They are **paternal half-brothers**.

**half-sister**: `/ˈhɑːf sɪstə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
A sister with whom you share one biological parent.
  - She met her half-sister for the first time last year.
  - She met her **half-sister** as an adult.
  - They are **maternal half-sisters**.

**stepbrother**: `/ˈstepbrʌðə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
The son of your stepparent who is not your biological brother.
  - Her stepbrother joined the family after their parents married.
  - He went camping with his **stepbrother**.

**stepsister**: `/ˈstepsɪstə(r)/` - `n. [C]`  
The daughter of your stepparent who is not your biological sister.
  - My stepsister and I share a room when we visit our father.
  - He lives with his mother, stepfather and **stepsister**.

**stepsibling**: `/ˈstepsɪblɪŋ/` - `n. [C]`  
A stepbrother or stepsister.
  - He grew up with a stepsibling from his mother's new marriage.
  - She has one **stepsibling** from her father's second marriage.
  - They developed a close relationship with their **stepsiblings**.

**sibling rivalry**: `/ˌsɪblɪŋ ˈraɪvəlri/` - `n. [U]`  
Competition, jealousy or conflict between brothers and sisters.
  - A little sibling rivalry is common when a new baby arrives.
  - The parents tried to reduce **sibling rivalry**.
  - A little **sibling rivalry** is common between brothers and sisters.

**elder**: `/ˈeldə(r)/` - `adj.`  
Older than another person, especially a brother or sister.
  - My elder brother helped me with my homework.
  - My **elder sister** is a doctor.
  - He has an **elder brother** and a younger sister.

**eldest**: `/ˈeldɪst/` - `adj.`  
Older than all the other children in a family.
  - Their eldest daughter is studying medicine.
  - Their **eldest child** is already married.
  - She is the **eldest of four children**.

## Idioms

**the black sheep (of the family)**
- **Meaning:** A family member regarded as different from the others, especially because their behaviour is disapproved of.
- **Register:** Neutral to informal.
- **Use:** Use when describing a family member seen as an outsider or as bringing embarrassment to the family.
- **Example:** He was considered **the black sheep of the family** because he rejected the career his relatives expected him to follow.
- **Caution:** It can sound judgemental or hurtful. Avoid applying it casually to someone in a sensitive conversation.

**be the spitting image of someone**
- **Meaning:** To look extremely similar to another person, especially a parent or other relative.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; common in British English.
- **Use:** Most often used for physical resemblance across generations.
- **Example:** At the same age, she was **the spitting image of her mother**.
- **Caution:** Primarily about appearance, not personality. “A dead ringer for someone” is a related informal idiom, but is not included as a separate entry here.

**be cut from the same cloth**
- **Meaning:** To be very similar in character, attitudes, or behaviour.
- **Register:** Neutral to semi-formal.
- **Use:** Can describe relatives who share a temperament or values, but also people who are not related.
- **Example:** The two brothers are **cut from the same cloth**: both are cautious, private, and fiercely independent.
- **Caution:** It can be approving or critical depending on the qualities being described.

## Proverbs and Proverbial Saying

**Children are a blessing.**
- **Meaning:** Children can bring great joy and value to a family.
- **Register:** Neutral to formal; often religious or sentimental depending on context.
- **Use:** Common in family celebrations and personal reflections, especially in communities where children are viewed as a blessing.
- **Example:** “The house feels so lively now.” “Children are a blessing.”
- **Caution:** This is a widely used proverbial statement rather than a fixed, uniquely English proverb. Avoid using it to dismiss someone’s struggles with parenting or infertility.

**You can choose your friends, but you can’t choose your family.**
- **Meaning:** Friendships are usually voluntary, whereas family relationships are assigned by birth or circumstance.
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Use:** Often said humorously or ruefully when relatives are difficult or very different from one another.
- **Example:** My cousins and I argue about everything, but you can choose your friends—you can’t choose your family.
- **Caution:** People can set boundaries and choose how much contact they have with relatives; the proverb does not mean someone must accept mistreatment.

## Expressions

**the little one(s)**
- **Meaning:** An affectionate way to refer to a young child or children.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2–B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** I'll need to leave by five to pick up the little ones.
- **Nuance:** Often used for children generally, not only babies.

## Practice: Dialogue, Monologue and Reading

### 1. Dialogue: A Busy Family Dinner

**Alex:** Are both your **parents** coming tonight?

**Mia:** Yes, my **mum** and **dad**. When I was little, I called them **mummy** and **daddy**.

**Alex:** Is your **stepfather** coming too?

**Mia:** Yes. My mum remarried, so he's my **step-parent**. I also have a **stepmother** on my dad's side.

**Alex:** How many children are in your family?

**Mia:** I'm an **only child**, but my partner has a **brother** and a **sister**. His **siblings** sometimes argue—classic **sibling rivalry**! He has a **half-brother**, a **half-sister**, a **stepbrother**, and a **stepsister**, so our family gatherings are lively.

**Alex:** Any twins?

**Mia:** Yes, my cousin has **identical twins**, and another relative has **non-identical twins**. There are also **triplets** in the wider family. I know the word **multiple** can refer to more than one baby from the same pregnancy; **quadruplets** are four babies.

**Alex:** What about your children?

**Mia:** We have a **son** and a **daughter**. They're our **offspring**—or, more formally, our **progeny**. My eldest is the **elder** of the two, and she's the **eldest** child in our household.

### 2. Monologue: My Immediate Family

My **immediate family** is a little complicated, but we're close. My **parents**, my **mother** and **father**, are often called **mum** and **dad**; when I was small, I said **mummy** and **daddy**. After they separated, both formed new relationships, so I also have a **stepmother** and a **stepfather**. My **step-parent** relationships have developed over time. I have two **siblings**, a **brother** and a **sister**, and like many families, we experienced some **sibling rivalry** growing up. My partner is an **only child**, while their family includes a **stepchild**, **stepson**, **stepdaughter**, **stepbrother**, **stepsister**, and **stepsibling** from blended-family relationships. Our relatives include **half-brothers** and **half-sisters** too. One branch of the family has **identical twins**, another has **non-identical twins**, and a cousin is raising **triplets**; four babies born together are **quadruplets**. In general, **multiple** births involve more than one baby. Our **children**—our **son** and **daughter**—are our **offspring**, or **progeny** in more formal language. The **elder** child is also the **eldest** in our household.

### 3. Reading: Terms for Close Family Relationships

Your **immediate family** commonly includes your **parents**, **children**, and **siblings**, although definitions can vary. A **parent** may be described as a **mother** or **father**; informal forms include **mum**, **dad**, **mummy**, and **daddy**. A **child** becomes plural as **children**; a male child is a **son**, and a female child is a **daughter**. **Offspring** is a general term, while **progeny** is more formal. A **sibling** is a **brother** or **sister**. A **half-brother** or **half-sister** shares one biological parent with you. A **step-parent** is married to or partnered with your parent but is not your biological parent; **stepmother** and **stepfather** specify gender. A **stepchild**, **stepson**, or **stepdaughter** is the child of one's spouse or partner from another relationship. **Stepbrother**, **stepsister**, and **stepsibling** describe relationships formed through a parent's marriage or partnership, not necessarily a biological connection. An **only child** has no siblings. **Sibling rivalry** describes competition or conflict between siblings. Twins may be **identical twins** or **non-identical twins**; a pregnancy involving more than one baby is a **multiple** pregnancy. Three babies born together are **triplets**, and four are **quadruplets**. **Elder** compares people within a family or group, while **eldest** identifies the oldest among three or more people.
