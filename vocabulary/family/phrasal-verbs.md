# Family-Related Phrasal Verbs

This file contains phrasal verbs with a direct, useful connection to family relationships, raising children, and caring for children. It deliberately excludes general life-stage vocabulary and expressions mainly about dating, marriage, or romantic relationships; those belong in their own topic files.

**Labels**
- **Priority:** **Core** = useful to learn and use; **Recognition** = especially useful to understand.
- **Level:** approximate CEFR guidance, not an official classification.
- Examples focus on family contexts. Some verbs also have other meanings outside this topic.

---

## Raising children and family care

### 1. bring someone up
- **Meaning:** To care for and raise a child until they are an adult.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Neutral; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** She was brought up by her grandparents.
- **Usage note:** In American English, “raise a child” is often more usual. “Bring up” can also mean introduce a topic.

### 2. look after someone
- **Meaning:** To take care of a child or family member.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2
- **Register:** Neutral; common in British English.
- **Example:** Could you look after the children while I go to the pharmacy?
- **Usage note:** “Look after” is especially common in British English; American English often uses “take care of”.

### 3. take care of someone
- **Meaning:** To provide the care or help a child or family member needs.
- **Priority / level:** Core · A2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** His grandmother took care of him while his parents were at work.
- **Usage note:** This is a verb phrase rather than a strict phrasal verb, but it is highly relevant to family care.

### 4. take someone in
- **Meaning:** To let a child or relative live in your home and care for them, often when they have nowhere else to go.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Neutral.
- **Example:** When her sister could no longer care for the boy, she took him in.
- **Usage note:** It can also mean understand or deceive someone, depending on context.

### 5. bring someone along
- **Meaning:** To bring a child or family member with you to an event or place.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** You can bring the kids along to the family picnic.
- **Usage note:** The phrase is general-purpose, but this is a common practical use in family plans.

### 6. babysit for someone
- **Meaning:** To look after someone's child for a short time while the parent or usual carer is away.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** My brother often babysits for us on Saturday evenings.
- **Usage note:** “Babysit” is the main verb; “sit for” is the phrasal-verb construction.

## Relationships within a family

### 7. take after someone
- **Meaning:** To resemble a parent or another relative in appearance or personality.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** She's very patient—she takes after her mum.
- **Usage note:** Usually refers to family resemblance, especially personality or appearance.

### 8. get on with someone
- **Meaning:** To have a friendly or good relationship with a relative.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1
- **Register:** Informal to neutral; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** I get on really well with my cousins.
- **Usage note:** “Get along with” is the common equivalent in American English.

### 9. fall out with someone
- **Meaning:** To have an argument with a relative and become unfriendly.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal; especially common in British English.
- **Example:** He fell out with his brother over their father's will.
- **Usage note:** It describes a disagreement that affects the relationship, not just a brief difference of opinion.

### 10. make up with someone
- **Meaning:** To become friendly again with a relative after an argument.
- **Priority / level:** Core · B1–B2
- **Register:** Informal.
- **Example:** She made up with her sister after they talked things through.
- **Usage note:** “Make up” alone can have other meanings; “make up with someone” clearly means reconcile.

### 11. cut someone off
- **Meaning:** To stop communicating with or supporting a relative.
- **Priority / level:** Recognition · B2
- **Register:** Informal to neutral.
- **Example:** After years of hurtful arguments, he cut off contact with his uncle.
- **Usage note:** “Cut someone off” can also mean interrupt them, so context matters. “Cut off contact with” makes the family meaning explicit.

## How to study this list

- Learn the child-care and family-relationship meanings first.
- Do not assume every item is used only in family contexts; the examples show the meanings relevant to this folder.
- Romantic relationships, marriage, and general life stages are intentionally outside the scope of this file.
