# Feedback works when people can act on it

*Illustrative LinkedIn article; adapt the ideas to your team and the type of work being reviewed.*

## Title

**Feedback works when people can act on it**

## Opening hook and relevance

“Be more proactive” sounds like feedback, but it leaves the recipient guessing. Does it mean raise risks earlier, make a recommendation, or complete a task without reminders? Feedback helps people improve when it identifies an observable behaviour and makes the next step clear.

## Context and central argument

In busy teams, feedback is often delivered quickly between tasks or saved for a formal review. Both settings can create problems if comments focus on personality rather than actions. Useful feedback describes what happened, why it mattered, and what could be tried next time. It should also leave room for the recipient’s perspective, because the reviewer may not know every constraint or dependency that shaped the result.

## Key insight 1 with evidence

Specificity makes feedback easier to use. Instead of saying “your update was unclear,” a manager could say, “The report listed the risks but did not identify who would follow up, so the team could not assign the next action.” The recipient can respond by adding an owner and due date to future updates. The comment is tied to the work, not a broad judgment about the person. It also explains the effect of the behaviour, which helps the recipient understand why the change matters.

## Key insight 2 or counterpoint

Feedback should not become a list of every possible flaw. Too many comments can make it difficult to identify the most important improvement, while vague praise can be pleasant but uninformative. Choose one or two priorities that matter for the task and invite a response. Ask whether the expectation was clear and whether the person had the resources to meet it. Accountability and curiosity can coexist: understanding a barrier does not mean ignoring the outcome.

## Practical implications

A simple feedback structure is: describe the situation, name the observable action, explain its impact, and agree on a next step. Follow up after the person has had a chance to apply the advice. If the change works, acknowledge it. If it does not, revisit the expectation rather than repeating the same comment more loudly. Written feedback should be concise and private when the topic is sensitive.

It is also important to distinguish a risk from a problem that has already occurred. A forecast may be uncertain because a dependency is still open; a missed deadline is a current issue that needs a response. Naming the difference helps managers choose the right action. A risk may need monitoring or a contingency, while an issue usually needs an owner and recovery plan. Teams can make this distinction without adding a complex reporting framework, as long as the terms are used consistently.

## Conclusion and conversation prompt

The test of feedback is not whether the reviewer has spoken, but whether the recipient knows what to do differently and why. What kind of feedback has helped you make a concrete improvement?
