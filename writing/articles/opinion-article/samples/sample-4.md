# Schools should teach practical media literacy

*Illustrative opinion article. Update examples to reflect current platforms and the school’s policies.*

## Headline

**Schools should teach practical media literacy**

## Opening and issue framing

Students can find information about almost any subject within seconds, but speed does not guarantee accuracy. A polished video may omit important context, a widely shared post may repeat an unverified claim, and a generated answer may sound confident without providing reliable evidence. Telling students simply to “check their sources” is not enough. Schools should teach a repeatable method for judging information and explaining uncertainty.

## Clear position and reasoning map

Media literacy should be taught as a practical skill across subjects rather than as a one-off lesson about internet safety. Students need to evaluate sources, recognise persuasive techniques, protect privacy, and understand how information can be edited or generated. The goal is not to make young people distrust everything; it is to help them decide what is supported, what remains uncertain, and what needs further checking.

## Reason 1 with support

Source evaluation improves when students practise on realistic examples. A class might compare two reports about the same event, identify the original source of a quotation, check publication dates, and ask whether independent sources support the central claim. Teachers can model how a conclusion changes when new evidence appears. This is more useful than memorising a list of “reliable” websites because even reputable sources can make mistakes or cover different aspects of an issue. Students should learn to look at authorship, evidence, purpose, and context together.

## Reason 2 with support

Media literacy also includes privacy and responsible sharing. Students should consider whether a post reveals someone’s location, whether an image can be shared with permission, and whether a headline fairly represents the content. When using AI tools, they should follow school rules, verify factual claims, and understand that generated text is not automatically original or accurate. These habits matter beyond school, including when students apply for jobs, participate in public debate, or make decisions about their own wellbeing.

## Counterargument and response

Some argue that the curriculum is already crowded and that digital skills are best learned at home. However, students do not all have equal access to guidance, and online platforms are closely connected to schoolwork and civic life. Media literacy can be integrated into existing lessons through short, subject-relevant tasks rather than added as a separate course in every year group.

Media literacy should be assessed through the reasoning students show, not through whether they reach a teacher’s preferred conclusion. A student may reasonably disagree with an interpretation while still demonstrating careful source checking and a fair account of the evidence. Teachers can ask learners to identify what would change their mind, which sources are independent, and which claims remain uncertain. This approach encourages intellectual humility without treating all claims as equally credible. It also makes room for discussion of how images, headlines, and recommendation systems shape what people notice.

## Conclusion and implication

Schools should make critical evaluation a normal part of learning. By practising source checks, privacy decisions, and transparent reasoning, students can become more confident users of information without confusing confidence with certainty.
