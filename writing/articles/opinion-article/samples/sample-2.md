# Cities should make walking and cycling safer

*Illustrative opinion article. Local infrastructure and safety claims should be checked against current evidence before publication.*

## Headline

**Cities should make walking and cycling safer**

## Opening and issue framing

A short journey does not always need a car, but people will not choose to walk or cycle if the route feels unsafe or disconnected. Painted lines that disappear at busy junctions, pavements blocked by parked vehicles, and crossings that require long waits can turn an otherwise practical trip into a stressful one. Encouraging active travel therefore requires more than asking residents to change their habits; it requires streets designed for the people who use them.

## Clear position and reasoning map

City authorities should make walking and cycling safe and continuous parts of the transport network. This is a practical investment in access, not simply a lifestyle campaign. The strongest case rests on giving residents realistic alternatives for short journeys and designing streets that work for children, older people, and people with disabilities as well as confident cyclists.

## Reason 1 with support

Safe infrastructure gives people a genuine choice. A cycle route that ends before a complicated intersection may be less useful than no route at all if riders must suddenly merge with fast traffic. Similarly, a narrow pavement can be difficult for wheelchair users, parents with strollers, and pedestrians carrying bags. Planning should therefore focus on complete journeys: protected space where traffic speeds are high, safe crossings at junctions, unobstructed pavements, and clear links to public transport. Routes should connect homes with schools, shops, parks, and workplaces rather than exist as isolated segments. This makes active travel more predictable and allows people to assess the route before they leave.

## Reason 2 with support

Better walking and cycling conditions can also improve neighbourhood life. When local trips can be made without driving, residents may spend less time searching for parking and more time using nearby services. Streets that are comfortable to cross can connect people with public spaces and local businesses. These outcomes depend on careful design and maintenance, not simply reducing vehicle access. Deliveries, emergency services, and people who need a vehicle must still be considered. A well-designed street balances different users instead of assuming that one mode should always take priority.

## Counterargument and response

Some residents argue that road space is limited and that cycling infrastructure can slow traffic or remove parking. These concerns deserve attention, especially where businesses depend on deliveries or public transport is weak. However, the answer is to assess each corridor, consult affected groups, and measure how the street functions after changes. Maintaining the current layout also has costs when it makes short journeys unsafe or forces everyone into a car.

## Conclusion and implication

Walking and cycling should be treated as transport options that need reliable infrastructure. Cities should prioritise continuous routes, safe junctions, accessible pavements, and maintenance, then use local feedback to improve the design rather than rely on awareness campaigns alone.
