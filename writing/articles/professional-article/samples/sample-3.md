# Making digital documents more accessible

*Illustrative professional article. Follow the relevant accessibility standards and organisational requirements when applying these recommendations.*

## Title

**Making digital documents more accessible**

## Executive opening

A document can look polished on screen and still be difficult to use with a screen reader, keyboard, or enlarged text. Accessibility is not a final cosmetic check; it depends on the structure and decisions made while the document is created. Teams can reduce avoidable barriers by using semantic structure, meaningful labels, and a review process that tests how people actually navigate the content.

## Context, definitions, and scope

This article focuses on common office documents and exported PDFs rather than the full accessibility of websites or software. The central principle is that information should remain understandable when visual appearance changes. Headings, lists, table headers, links, and reading order communicate structure to assistive technologies and help all readers scan the document. Specific legal or technical requirements vary by jurisdiction and organisation, so teams should consult the applicable standard rather than assume that one checklist is universally sufficient.

## Professional insight 1

Use built-in heading styles instead of making text look like a heading only by increasing its font size or bolding it. Semantic headings create a navigable outline and help readers understand how sections relate. Keep heading levels in a logical order; choosing a level solely because its appearance looks right can create a confusing structure. Lists should use actual list formatting, and tables should be reserved for information that benefits from rows and columns rather than used to position page elements.

Link text should explain its destination. A phrase such as “read the application guidance” gives more context than “click here,” especially when links are read out of surrounding text. Images that communicate information need meaningful alternative text, while decorative images should not add noise to the reading experience. These choices are easier to make during drafting than to repair after a document has been circulated.

## Professional insight 2

Automated checkers are useful for identifying some common issues, but they cannot judge every aspect of accessibility. A checker may detect missing alternative text without deciding whether the text actually describes the image’s purpose. It may flag a heading structure but not establish whether the content is understandable. Human review should therefore include keyboard navigation, reading order, meaningful link labels, table structure, and a check that the document still makes sense when zoomed or read aloud.

Accessibility also benefits from plain language. Long sentences, unexplained abbreviations, and inconsistent terminology make a document harder to use for many readers, not only people using assistive technology. Clear structure and concise writing should be treated as part of the same quality process.

## Recommendations and application

Build accessibility checks into the document workflow. Use styles from the start, write descriptive link text, provide appropriate alternative text, and check table headers. Run the available checker, then review the document manually and test exported formats rather than assuming the source file and PDF behave identically. Assign responsibility for remediation and keep a record of unresolved issues when a document cannot be fixed immediately.

## Risks, limitations, and open questions

No single checklist guarantees accessibility for every user or context. Complex documents may need specialist review and testing with people who use assistive technologies. Teams should also avoid collecting personal information about disability unnecessarily when asking for feedback.

Testing with users should be part of the document workflow, not a one-time sign-off. A document may be accessible in one application and become less usable after conversion, compression, or publication to a different format. The team should define who checks the source file, who checks the final export, and how reported barriers are prioritised. Where a complex document is essential to a public service, specialist review may be necessary. The effort should be proportionate to the document’s importance and audience, but it should not be skipped merely because the document passes an automated score.

## Conclusion

Accessible documents are built through structure, language, and testing rather than appearance alone. Making these checks part of normal production improves usability and reduces the cost of fixing barriers later.
